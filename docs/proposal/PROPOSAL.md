# Handrail: a floor and a path

Parents in control. One plan for every screen.

Handrail is a proposed open standard, with a certification mark, for children’s screen time. It is not an app. It is a written plan for each child, a common language that every certified device and service speaks, and a mark that says a product speaks it honestly. The law sets a minimum age and that stays as the floor. Above it, a parent sets one plan for the household, once, from the app the family already uses or from any certified alternative. Every certified device, console, app and platform follows that plan and reports back what it actually did. The child sees every rule and the reason for it.

Draft 0.5, 22 October 2026. Written for ministers, officials and regulators in Sweden, where this will be proposed first, and for anyone else who is about to legislate a minimum age for social media or screens. It is a design, not a product. It contains no code and no demo. Diagrams are specified in DIAGRAMS.md and imagined screens in VISUALS.md. A technical draft of the data model exists separately and is not needed to judge the argument. Material for funders lives in FUNDING-LANDSCAPE.md. No author is named in this draft.

## Executive summary

**What a parent gets.** Handrail is for every screen a child uses, from a first tablet at two to a first phone, not only for social media. Set the rules once. Two hours a day, and it means two hours in total across the tablet, the console and the phone, not two hours on each. School apps only on a school day until three o’clock. Or homework time, where education apps count and games wait. Bedtime at nine, phone out of the bedroom. Then it holds, on every screen in the house, whoever made it. Apple keeps Screen Time, Google keeps Family Link, Sony keeps its app. Once certified, any one of them can set the plan for the whole household and read back what the others did, and a family that prefers an independent app, website or home hub can use that instead. The child sees the same rules in the same words, with the reason beside each one. A plan says how long, what and when: which apps and sites are allowed, which sites, topics and words are blocked, and when each applies. A parent or guardian switches it on by choice, on the family account they already have, and can switch it off the same way.

**Why today does not work.** A parent of two runs four or five separate parental apps today. None sees the others. Family Link counts per device. Nothing on an iPhone reaches a Switch or a PlayStation. Of eleven vendor tools checked for this paper, nine can set a daily limit and not one can see another vendor’s device or let a parent join them up (section 2a). In Sweden, 92 per cent of parents know the Public Health Agency’s hour limits, yet 85 per cent worry they cannot protect their child online and one in five do not know how ([Internetstiftelsen, December 2025](https://internetstiftelsen.se/om-oss/press/pressmeddelanden/svenska-foraldrar-oroar-sig-for-att-inte-kunna-skydda-sitt-barn-pa-natet/)). Knowing the limit and applying it across five apps are different things.

**Why a ban alone is not the answer.** Sweden’s inquiry, SOU 2026:35, proposes that no child holds a social media account before the year they turn 15, from 1 January 2028 ([Regeringen, June 2026](https://www.regeringen.se/rattsliga-dokument/statens-offentliga-utredningar/2026/06/sou-202635/)). This paper does not ask Sweden to drop that. It asks Sweden to look at what a bare ban does. Australia has run the experiment. Three months in, 81 per cent of 10 to 15 year olds still used a restricted platform, from 86 before; about one in ten said a parent helped them ([eSafety via Engadget, July 2026](https://engadget.com/2227908/australia-social-media-ban-for-kids-under-16-impact-so-far)). The commonest fix at home is a parent’s adult account or a new account with an adult birthday, and at that moment every teen protection the platform built disappears: private by default, strangers cannot message, sleep mode, time reminders (section 2.5). A ban does not remove the child. It removes the protection. It also makes every adult prove their age, pushes children to apps the ban does not cover, and turns enforcement into a chase that never ends. The Chancellor of Justice, Bris, the Children’s Ombudsman and the children the inquiry heard all said the same thing in the consultation: a limit alone is not enough, and the inquiry did not weigh alternatives (section 1a).

**What Handrail adds.** Keep the statutory age as the floor. Where the law sets an age, certified platforms apply it to child accounts as the law requires, and Handrail adds no age check of its own. Above the floor, a parent-managed account carries the household’s own plan, loosening step by step as the child shows they can stop, choose and judge. Platforms receive only the parts of the plan they need to apply it, under a code that is different for every platform. They never receive a name, a date of birth, a document, messages or location. The child’s record stays in the home and is deleted after 31 days by default. An AI guide is optional. A household can run with no guide at all. Where a parent switches one on, it runs in sentry mode (sees only what the parent ticks, suggests, never acts) or control mode (acts only within limits the parent sets, every action shown to the child). In no mode does it see messages, browsing, location or the child’s name (section 3.5).

**What we ask of Sweden.** Four things, all possible whichever government forms. One clause in the bill that follows the inquiry: a parent-managed account carrying a certified household plan meets the platform’s duty, with a safe harbour for a platform that applies the plan honestly. Ask the Public Health Agency to publish its hours by age as the default Swedish plan. Sponsor a pilot of 400 Swedish households with an independent evaluation, published whatever it finds, at about 3.5 million EUR for the whole programme. And before the Commission’s KIDS Act feedback closes on 3 December 2026, ask that the proposed parent-controlled account for 13 and 14 year olds can be run through any certified plan, from any vendor (section 7).

**The bet.** If Sweden builds proper parental controls first, it may never need to fight its own children over a ban. The EU may still push one. That is the next fight, and Sweden would argue it from evidence rather than from hope.

## 1. The problem as you feel it

You need three things: a sentence in law, a list of the services it covers and a fine for breaking it. Many parents say they cannot control how long their children spend on screens. In Sweden the inquiry SOU 2026:35 proposed in June 2026 that no child holds a social media account before the year they turn 15, with platforms responsible for checking age and no penalties for children or parents ([Regeringen, June 2026](https://www.regeringen.se/pressmeddelanden/2026/06/utredning-foreslar-aldersgrans-for-sociala-medier/)). Section 1a sets out the Swedish position in full. Sweden is not alone. The UK consultation drew about 116,000 responses. Among the parents and carers whose answers were broken out, around nine in ten backed a minimum age of 16 ([Jurist, June 2026](https://www.jurist.org/news/2026/06/starmer-sets-spring-2027-target-for-under-16-social-media-ban/); [Medianama, June 2026](https://www.medianama.com/2026/06/223-why-uk-banning-social-media-under-16s-takeaways-public-consultation/)). Australia has acted. At least fifteen countries have enacted, passed or announced age limits ([TechCrunch, July 2026](https://techcrunch.com/2026/07/21/social-media-ban-children-countries-list/)). In September 2026 the European Commission proposed, in the KIDS Act, a minimum age of 15 for holding an independent online account ([Commission, EU age verification](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/eu-age-verification)). In the UK the powers now exist in section 214A of the Online Safety Act, inserted by section 70 of the Children’s Wellbeing and Schools Act 2026 ([legislation.gov.uk](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2026/21/section/70); [DLA Piper, April 2026](https://www.dlapiper.com/en-hu/insights/publications/2026/04/online-safety-act)).

A ban gives you the sentence, the list and the fine. This paper does not ask you to give any of them up. It asks you to add a second route that gives you the same three things, costs fewer adults their privacy, survives a court, and teaches a child something before the age the law names.

## 1a. Sweden, where this is proposed first

Sweden is further on than most countries. It has national guidance, a proposed floor, a school phone ban, and a consultation that has just asked for something beside a ban. What it lacks is the tool that lets a parent apply any of it across the screens a child actually uses.

**The guidance.** On 2 September 2024 the Public Health Agency (Folkhälsomyndigheten) published screen time recommendations by age: no screens under 2, at most 1 hour a day at 2 to 5, 1 to 2 hours at 6 to 12, 2 to 3 hours at 13 to 18, no screens before bed and phones out of the bedroom at night. It also advised waiting until 13 before a child gets a smartphone ([Regeringen, September 2024](https://regeringen.se/pressmeddelanden/2024/09/rekommendationer-om-digital-medieanvandning-for-barn-och-unga/)). On 1 June 2026 the agency added advice for parents’ own habits: put the phone away when you are with your child, agree phone-free zones at home, think before posting pictures of your child ([Folkhälsomyndigheten, June 2026](https://www.folkhalsomyndigheten.se/nyheter-och-press/nyhetsarkiv/2026/juni/folkhalsomyndigheten-presenterar-nya-rekommendationer-om-foraldrars-skarmvanor/)). The guidance has moved behaviour. Mediemyndigheten’s Ungar & medier 2025 found that 9 to 12 year olds spend 40 minutes a day less on digital devices than in 2022, and that the share of 9 year olds with no mobile at all has nearly doubled ([Mediemyndigheten via TT, September 2025](https://via.tt.se/pressmeddelande/4064171/ny-statistik-anvandningen-av-digitala-medier-minskar-bland-yngre-barn?publisherId=3235838&lang=sv)). The Public Health Agency credits the recommendations. Guidance plus parents already works. The problem is that parents cannot apply it across vendors. Internetstiftelsen found 92 per cent of parents know the limits, 39 per cent say they changed nothing, 85 per cent worry they cannot protect their child online and one in five do not know how. Rows about screen time peak in mellanstadiet (ages 10 to 12), where 58 per cent of parents report them ([Internetstiftelsen, December 2025](https://internetstiftelsen.se/om-oss/press/pressmeddelanden/svenska-foraldrar-oroar-sig-for-att-inte-kunna-skydda-sitt-barn-pa-natet/)). A Swedish household plan can start from the Public Health Agency’s hours by age and let the parent change what they like (section 4.2).

**The inquiry.** On 9 October 2025 the government appointed an inquiry (Dir. 2025:91) led by Lisa Englund Krafft to analyse an age limit for social media and to follow the EU’s age verification work ([Riksdagen, Dir. 2025:91](https://www.riksdagen.se/sv/dokument-och-lagar/dokument/kommittedirektiv/en-aldersgrans-till-skydd-for-barns-halsa-och_hdb191/)). Its interim report, SOU 2026:35 of 2 June 2026, proposes a law barring logged-in social media accounts before the calendar year a child turns 15, in force 1 January 2028, with platforms responsible for checking age under the DSA. There is no parental opt-in, because the inquiry judged that parental consent would make protection unequal and expose parents to peer pressure. There are no penalties for children or parents, because children must dare to tell adults what happened ([Regeringen, SOU 2026:35](https://www.regeringen.se/rattsliga-dokument/statens-offentliga-utredningar/2026/06/sou-202635/)). The inquiry’s own text records that platforms do not check age at sign-up and that existing parental controls are limited and depend on linking a teen account to a parent account. It also records what the children it heard said: a limit is easy to get round, will not be followed, and will move children to less regulated platforms, and parents and school should give children support and tools instead ([SOU 2026:35, pp 67 to 69](https://www.regeringen.se/contentassets/d1b21d5210774bfba9797e08883c9633/sou-2026_35_pdf-a_webb.pdf)). Age verification is left to the final report, due 14 November 2026 ([Regeringen, remiss page](https://www.regeringen.se/remisser/2026/06/remiss-sou-202635-en-aldersgrans-for-barns-tillgang-till-sociala-medier/)). Separately, a national mobile phone ban in compulsory schools took effect on 1 August 2026: phones are collected at the start of the school day and handed back at the end ([Skolverket, June 2026](https://www.skolverket.se/om-skolverket/nyheter-och-pressmeddelanden/nyheter/nyheter/2026-06-17-flera-regelandringar-for-trygghet-sakerhet-och-studiero-borjar-galla-i-sommar)).

**The consultation.** The remiss on SOU 2026:35 closed on 16 October 2026. Read together, the responses ask for something beside a ban. The Chancellor of Justice (Justitiekanslern) advised against the proposal, calling it a significant intrusion into children’s fundamental rights and criticising the inquiry for proposing a limit without any closer consideration of alternatives ([Dagens Juridik, October 2026](https://www.dagensjuridik.se/nyheter/jk-sager-nej-till-aldersgrans-pa-sociala-medier/)). Bris neither supports nor opposes the limit, says a ban alone will not keep children safe, warns that some children will look for ways round it or move to less safe places, and says the main responsibility now lies with the platforms ([Bris via TT, October 2026](https://via.tt.se/pressmeddelande/4598937/bris-aldersgrans-pa-sociala-medier-racker-inte?publisherId=1239792&lang=sv)). The Children’s Ombudsman (Barnombudsmannen) backs a limit in principle but says it can never answer all the risks and must be paired with support to children and parents ([Barnombudsmannen, September 2026](https://www.regeringen.se/contentassets/9749352ad9724cd89625ad26807ea933/barnombudsmannen.pdf)). Internetstiftelsen rejects the proposal in its current form, citing circumvention, migration to less regulated services and the privacy cost of making every adult prove their age ([Internetstiftelsen, October 2026](https://www.regeringen.se/contentassets/9749352ad9724cd89625ad26807ea933/internetstiftelsen.pdf)). Mediemyndigheten accepts a limit but says it cannot alone handle the risks and must be combined with support to parents and school. Researchers and mental health groups add that a ban hits vulnerable young people hardest ([NSPH, October 2026](https://nsph.se/2026/10/15/aldersgrans-for-sociala-medier-far-inte-sla-hardast-mot-de-mest-sarbara/)). None of these bodies is against protecting children. They are asking what sits beside the floor.

**Who owns it.** The age limit and the Public Health Agency’s work sit with the Minister for Social Affairs, Jakob Forssmed (KD), who commissioned both. The school phone ban sits with the Minister for Education, Simona Mohamsson (L). The state e-ID, available from age 9 from 1 December 2026 and the basis for Sweden’s EU digital identity wallet, sits with the Minister for Civil Affairs, Erik Slottner (KD) ([Regeringen, December 2025](https://www.regeringen.se/pressmeddelanden/2025/12/expertgrupp-utsedd-till-utredningen-om-en-aldersgrans-pa-sociala-medier/)). Mediemyndigheten, under the culture ministry, is Sweden’s DSA authority. As of 21 October 2026 all of these are caretaker ministers: the 13 September election produced no majority, and government formation is unresolved. The limit has cross-party support, including from the Social Democrats, who proposed their own strict 15 limit in October 2025. Whichever government forms, a 15 limit is likely to proceed. This paper is written for the officials in Socialdepartementet who will draft the bill, and for whoever holds the portfolio when they do.

**The EU pressure.** Sweden has leaned into EU action. It offered experts to the Commission’s child safety panel in October 2025 ([Regeringen, October 2025](https://www.regeringen.se/pressmeddelanden/2025/10/sverige-vill-bidra-med-experter-till-eus-utredningar-av-sociala-medier/)) and was among the 25 EU states that signed the Jutland Declaration calling for EU-level age verification (inferred: only Estonia and Belgium declined) ([Heise, October 2025](https://www.heise.de/en/news/EU-states-favour-social-media-ban-for-children-few-dissenters-10750803.html)). On 17 September 2026 the Commission proposed the EU KIDS Act: no accounts under 13, a parent-controlled account at 13 and 14 with a one-hour daily limit and approved contacts, an own account from 15, certified age verification, and fines up to 6 per cent of turnover ([Commission, KIDS Act FAQ](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/faqs/kids-act-explained)). One law-firm reading of the text says member states could not set a higher age than the EU does; this is unverified against the articles and conflicts with the Commission panel’s July report ([DWT, September 2026](https://www.dwt.com/blogs/privacy--security-law-blog/2026/09/eu-kids-act-online-child-safety-proposal)). The Commission has already told France that a national ban that duplicates DSA duties breaches EU law, and France’s constitutional court then struck the French law down ([AFP via The Star, July 2026](https://www.thestar.com.my/tech/tech-news/2026/07/07/eu-tells-france-to-amend-social-media-ban-law)). The lesson for Sweden is that a national age law must sit on top of the DSA and leave platform duties to Brussels. Feedback on the KIDS Act is open until 3 December 2026 ([Brussels Times, October 2026](https://www.brusselstimes.com/2343893/eu-kids-act-draft-sparks-widespread-feedback-push-to-reshape-digital-childhoods)).

**Why doing it properly first matters.** The KIDS Act’s parent-controlled account for 13 and 14 year olds, with a daily limit and approved contacts set by a guardian, is a household plan by another name. Nobody has said how it works across vendors. Sweden has national hours by age, a measured fall in younger children’s screen time, a proposed floor, and a consultation asking for alternatives. It can show how a parent-controlled tier works across Apple, Google, Sony, Nintendo, Microsoft and the social platforms before the EU fixes the rules. If proper parental controls arrive first, and the evidence shows families using them, Sweden may never need to enforce a ban against its own children. The honest caveat: the EU may still legislate a ban, and under the DSA Sweden could not stop it. That is the next fight. A working alternative with Swedish evidence behind it is the strongest position to argue from, and the only one that does not depend on hoping the chase will one day end.

## 2. What a ban costs, on the evidence

The evidence is early. Most of it was measured three months after Australia’s ban commenced. It already points one way: the ban leaks, checks adults, teaches nothing, and turns parents into the people who break it.

### 2.1 Bans leak

Australia’s under-16 law commenced on 10 December 2025. Platforms removed or restricted 4.7 million accounts in the first half of December, a preliminary count of accounts rather than children ([eSafety, January 2026](https://esafety.gov.au/newsroom/media-releases/platforms-restrict-access-to-47-million-under-16-accounts-across-australia)). Two later surveys give different figures because they count different groups. In March 2026 a pulse survey of 898 parents found that, for Instagram, Snapchat and TikTok, around seven in ten children who had an account before the law still had one; overall, parents reporting any account fell from about 50 to 31 per cent ([eSafety, March 2026](https://www.esafety.gov.au/sites/default/files/2026-03/SocialMediaMinimumAgeComplianceUpdateMarch2026.pdf)). In July 2026 eSafety published a survey of more than 4,000 children and families, measured in March 2026, which found that over 81 per cent of 10 to 15 year olds were still using at least one restricted platform, down from about 86 per cent before the law, with daily use moving from 60 to 58 per cent and account ownership from 52 to 42 per cent ([Al Jazeera, August 2026](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/8/1/most-australian-teens-still-online-three-months-after-under-16-ban); [Reuters via TBS News, July 2026](https://www.tbsnews.net/tech/most-australian-teens-still-social-media-three-months-after-under-16-ban-began-study-finds)). Around half of children who kept accounts said the platform never checked their age. A separate BMJ study of 408 children in New South Wales found over 85 per cent still using covered platforms three months in, mostly through accounts they already held, with almost a fifth using a fake account, and reported "insufficient evidence to conclude that exposure to the Act had any early substantial effects" ([The National, June 2026](https://www.thenationalnews.com/future/technology/2026/06/25/age-verification-flaws-undermine-australias-social-media-ban-for-under-16s/)). Removed accounts are not removed children.

### 2.2 Bans verify adults

A minimum age can only be enforced by checking everyone. Ofcom counted over 69 million age checks on 32 UK services in the second half of 2025 ([Ofcom, July 2026](https://www.ofcom.org.uk/siteassets/resources/documents/online-safety/information-for-industry/age-assurance-report-2026/report-on-the-use-of-age-assurance.pdf)). France’s Conseil constitutionnel struck down the under-15 law on 14 August 2026, mainly as a disproportionate limit on freedom of expression and communication: it reached services whose risks were not established, ignored age and maturity, and gave parents no way to lift it. It also found that forcing every user, adults included, to prove their age with no legal conditions or limits breached the right to private life ([LCP, August 2026](https://lcp.fr/actualites/reseaux-sociaux-pourquoi-le-conseil-constitutionnel-a-censure-l-interdiction-pour-les)). A US federal judge compared Texas’s app-store age law to making every bookshop check every customer at the door ([9to5Mac, December 2025](https://9to5mac.com/2025/12/23/texas-judge-blocks-state-app-store-age-verification-law/)). About 850 million people worldwide have no ID at all ([EFF, October 2026](https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2026/10/when-no-id-means-no-internet-age-verification-and-right-access-information)).

### 2.3 The data leaks

A Discord support vendor exposed government ID photos collected for age-verification appeals in Discord’s UK and Australia pilot: about 70,000 by Discord’s count, disputed by the attackers, who claimed far more ([The Record, October 2025](https://therecord.media/discord-government-docs-exposed-breach); [The Register, October 2025](https://www.theregister.com/2025/10/09/discord_photo_ids_leaked/)). The Tea app leaked about 13,000 verification selfies it had promised to delete ([The Record, July 2025](https://therecord.media/tea-app-data-breach-stolen-ids-leaked)). Australia’s own technology trial found vendors retaining data in "over-anticipation" of regulators' needs, and found facial estimation wrongly rejecting 8.5 per cent of 16 year olds at a 16+ gate, with worse accuracy for women, darker skin and Indigenous users ([Australian Government, August 2025](https://www.infrastructure.gov.au/department/media/publications/age-assurance-technology-trial-final-report)).

### 2.4 People route around

When UK age checks went live on 25 July 2025, Proton VPN reported UK sign-ups up more than 1,400 per cent against baseline within hours of the rules taking effect ([The Register, July 2025](https://www.theregister.com/2025/07/28/uk_vpn_demand_soars/)). When Australia’s adult-site checks began on 9 March 2026, Sensor Tower data showed Proton VPN moving from 174th to 19th and NordVPN from 189th to 13th on the iPhone free chart within days ([TechSpot, March 2026](https://www.techspot.com/news/111607-vpn-downloads-surge-australia-after-age-checks-hit.html); [TechRadar, March 2026](https://www.techradar.com/vpn/vpn-privacy-security/vpns-surge-in-australia-as-mandatory-age-verification-for-adult-content-begins)). Figures are vendor-reported, and should be read as direction rather than magnitude.

### 2.5 Workarounds remove the protections

The holes in a ban are not only clever teenagers. Parents make many of them, because their child’s friends are all there and because nobody is punished for it. Australia’s eSafety evaluation three months in found that, of children still on a restricted platform, about half were never asked their age, just over a third had an account age set over 16, about 11 per cent said a parent helped them, and 7.4 per cent used a VPN ([eSafety via Engadget, July 2026](https://engadget.com/2227908/australia-social-media-ban-for-kids-under-16-impact-so-far)). Parents were helping from the first week: a 13 year old told Crikey, "I just put my mum’s face in for the facial verification and it worked"; a 14 year old’s mother stays logged in to her TikTok; a father said he saw no problem as long as parents are happy ([Crikey, December 2025](https://www.crikey.com.au/?p=1230975)). Four months in, 61 per cent of 12 to 15 year olds who had accounts before still had one, 70 per cent of them called getting round the ban easy, and 7 to 10 per cent had a friend or family member set up a new over-16 account for them ([Molly Rose Foundation, April 2026](https://mollyrosefoundation.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/MRF_Australia-Social-Media-Ban-Research_Briefing-April-26.pdf)). In the UK, 39 per cent of 11 to 17 year olds have beaten an age check; 11 per cent of those who posed as older used a parent’s or relative’s ID; 10 per cent learned the trick from a parent; and of children who use a VPN, 22 per cent were helped by a parent to set it up and 43 per cent have a parent paying for it ([DSIT and BMG Research, July 2026](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/childrens-circumvention-behaviours-online)). Before any ban, a third of UK children aged 8 to 17 with a profile already had an adult user age set, and up to two thirds of 8 to 12 year olds had a parent’s help setting up the profile ([Ofcom via LBC, October 2022](https://www.ofcom.org.uk/news-centre/2022/a-third-of-children-have-false-social-media-age-of-18)).

**What the child loses.** This is the danger nobody says out loud. The moment a child moves onto a parent’s account, or a new account with an adult birthday, every protection the platform built for teenagers disappears. An Instagram teen account is private by default, can be messaged only by people the teen already follows, carries a 13+ sensitive content filter, the strictest hidden words, a 60-minute daily reminder and a sleep mode from 22:00 to 07:00, and a 13 to 15 year old needs a parent’s permission to loosen any of it ([Meta Family Center](https://familycenter.meta.com/instagram-safety/)). An under-16 TikTok account has no direct messaging at all, is private by default, is excluded from the For You feed and carries a 60-minute default limit ([TikTok to Oklahoma Senate, October 2024](https://oksenate.gov/sites/default/files/2024-10/TikTok%20Letter%20to%20Oklahoma%20SJC%2010.17.24.pdf)). YouTube’s under-18 defaults turn on bedtime and take-a-break reminders, turn off autoplay and make uploads private ([YouTube, January 2026](https://blog.youtube/inside-youtube/a-decade-of-building-for-teens-and-families/)). An adult account has none of this. The inference that a borrowed adult account carries no teen protection is ours, not a statement by any vendor, but it follows from how every vendor describes its own settings. A ban does not remove the child. It removes the protection.

**Where the children go.** The ban moved children to services it does not cover. eSafety measured messaging-service use rising from 40.5 to 52.3 per cent, games from 26.4 to 29 per cent and Pinterest from 16.6 to 21.8 per cent ([eSafety via Engadget, July 2026](https://engadget.com/2227908/australia-social-media-ban-for-kids-under-16-impact-so-far)). Discord, exempt from the ban, saw its popularity among Australian teens rise while facing lawsuits over child predators ([ChannelNews, January 2026](https://www.channelnews.com.au/soccial-media-platform-benefitting-from-teen-bans-in-oz-looks-to-benefit-with-ipo/)). Meta said in December 2025 it was seeing teens move to apps lacking teen accounts. AI companions sit outside the ban entirely; the four eSafety examined lacked meaningful age assurance and did not redirect suicide or self-harm prompts to human help ([UTS, March 2026](https://www.uts.edu.au/news/2026/03/when-ai-companionship-turns-dark)). The children the Swedish inquiry heard predicted exactly this: an age limit would move them to other, probably less regulated, platforms ([SOU 2026:35, section 4.5](https://www.regeringen.se/contentassets/d1b21d5210774bfba9797e08883c9633/sou-2026_35_pdf-a_webb.pdf)).

**What the child stops saying.** Researchers warn that a child on a banned platform becomes less likely to tell a parent about something frightening, because they believe they should not be there at all ([Euronews, January 2026](https://euronews.com/next/2026/01/29/australias-under-16-social-media-ban-how-children-are-already-finding-ways-around-it)). That is expert reasoning, not yet a measured effect; the only measured link found is a Greek study in which 60 per cent of secondary pupils said they would hesitate to tell parents about an upsetting experience in a game, chiefly for fear of being banned from it ([Better Internet for Kids, March 2020](https://better-internet-for-kids.europa.eu/en/news/safer-internet-day-study-reveals-greek-children-dont-discuss-online-safety-parents)). Sweden’s inquiry made the same point when it chose no penalties for children or parents: children must dare to tell. A plan the child can read, with reasons, is built to keep that door open. A ban the child is quietly breaking closes it.

**The enforcement war.** The money goes on the chase. Australia’s eSafety received A$16.2 million for the minimum-age programme in 2025/26 within a four-year allocation of A$45.7 million ([eSafety FOI LOG 161, January 2026](https://www.esafety.gov.au/sites/default/files/2026-01/LOG_161_Document_Set.pdf)); Ofcom’s Online Safety Act spend reached £64.1 million in 2024/25. Australia’s own technology trial found facial estimation wrongly rejecting 8.5 per cent of 16 year olds, with worse results for darker skin, women and Indigenous users ([Squire Patton Boggs, September 2025](https://www.squirepattonboggs.com/insights/publications/new-report-tests-digital-age-checks-in-australia/)), and teenagers defeated face scans with a parent’s face, a photo of a dog or a mesh mask. In June 2026 Australia moved to double the maximum fine to A$99 million because, in the Prime Minister’s words, there were still too many children on social media. Each new rule produces a new workaround, which produces a new check, which produces a new workaround. There is no exit from that loop. Every krona spent in it is a krona not spent giving parents a tool that works. Sweden’s inquiry has already accepted that children and parents should not be punished. That leaves platforms to police a rule that the families around the child are helping to break. The alternative is to give those families something they would rather use.

### 2.6 The cliff edge

A ban teaches nothing. A child is kept out until a birthday, then admitted, unprepared, into the same product. The UN Committee on the Rights of the Child asks states to respect children’s "evolving capacities" as an enabling principle, to help parents favour "mutual empathy and respect, over prohibition or control", to teach digital literacy from preschool, and to limit "robust age verification" to goods and services illegal for children ([UNCRC General Comment 25, paras 19, 86, 104, 114](https://www.ohchr.org/en/documents/general-comments-and-recommendations/general-comment-no-25-2021-childrens-rights-relation)). The UK’s own Children’s Code protects children "within" the digital world, not from it ([ICO](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/childrens-information/childrens-code-guidance-and-resources/age-appropriate-design-a-code-of-practice-for-online-services/)). 5Rights warns that a social-media-only ban displaces children to games, AI chatbots and EdTech ([5Rights, February 2026](https://5rightsfoundation.com/access-limitations-must-be-part-of-age-appropriate-design-and-effectively-restrict-companies-from-exploiting-children/)).

Two things this paper does not argue. It does not argue that screens are harmless. The research on harm is contested, and a summary of both sides is in Appendix A. Handrail does not need screens to be harmful to be worth doing. It needs childhood to be a time of learning. And it does not argue that parents are wrong to want a ban. They want their children safe and the fighting to stop. A ban promises both. The question is whether it can deliver them alone.

## 2a. Where parents are today

A parent of two children with a tablet, a games console and a phone holds four or five separate apps: Google Family Link, Apple Screen Time, Microsoft Family Safety, and the Nintendo and PlayStation parent apps. None of them can see the others. Family Link counts time per device, so two hours means two hours on each. Microsoft removed the instant lock from Family Safety without notice. The PlayStation Family app has no interface beyond the console and the app itself. Apple’s Screen Time lives on the device and speaks to nothing outside it. No vendor publishes a parental-control interface a household can use; the open-source community reaches two of them only through private web endpoints that breach the vendors' terms and stop working when the vendor changes them (see the platform matrix in docs/PLATFORMS.md).

Of eleven vendor tools checked for this paper on 21 October 2026 (Apple, Google, Microsoft, Nintendo, Sony, Instagram, TikTok, YouTube, Roblox, Discord and Family Link’s school-time mode), nine can set a daily limit, five can set a real bedtime or scheduled block, one comes close to an "education only" rule, none can see use on another vendor’s device, and none exposes an interface another parent app could use to read or write the plan ([Apple](https://support.apple.com/en-gb/105121); [Google](https://support.google.com/families/answer/15938652?hl=en); [Microsoft](https://www.microsoft.com/en-gb/microsoft-365/family-safety); [Nintendo](https://www.nintendo.com/en-gb/Support/Nintendo-Switch/How-to-Set-Up-or-Adjust-Nintendo-Switch-Parental-Controls-1494771.html); [PlayStation, September 2025](https://blog.playstation.com/2025/09/10/announcing-playstation-family-app-for-parental-controls-and-family-management/)). Each app offers the same basic controls: a daily limit, a bedtime, an allow list, a lock. None has a notion of a child, only of a device or an account. None can grant minutes earned on one screen for use on another, and none can tell a parent what another vendor did. The household’s plan, where one exists at all, lives in a parent’s head.

Handrail does not replace these apps, and it is not a sixth one. Each vendor keeps its own app and its own design. What changes is that every vendor’s service speaks one plan and returns one result, so a budget set once holds across the console, the tablet and the phone, and a parent sees a single honest picture. To carry the mark, a device or service must honour the plan, read back what it did and keep the child’s record in the home.

**Two ways to use it.** A parent can stay with the app they already have. Once Screen Time or Family Link is certified as a controller, it can set the plan for the Switch, the PlayStation and the Windows laptop as well as its own devices, and show what each of them did. Or a parent can use an independent controller: an app, a website or a home hub from anyone who passes the test, by hand or automated. The vendors’ services are the same in both cases. Each must accept a plan from any certified controller, may not insist that the parent uses its own app, and may not give another company’s controller fewer controls than its own. Handrail itself puts no app in front of parents. It publishes the standard, the test suite and open-source reference software that any builder can start from.

## 3. The alternative: a floor and a path

### 3.1 How the two fit together

The statutory minimum age stays. It becomes the default policy that every certified platform applies to every child account that carries no household plan. A parent who never engages, never installs anything and never makes a decision gets exactly what the law would have given them. That is the answer to the hard case that drives the ban politically: the child whose parents will not engage is covered by the floor, automatically, on every certified platform.

One limit. Applying the floor to an account requires the platform to know the account belongs to a child. Handrail gives the platform two signals: a plan, or a parent-declared OS age band on a family account. For an account that carries neither, Handrail removes nothing of the age-assurance problem the ban has. Handrail replaces verification for parent-managed accounts. Where an account has a parent-declared OS age band but no plan, it relies on the regulator accepting that band as enough. It does not by itself remove the pressure to verify unmanaged, adult-looking accounts. Section 7 asks for that acceptance explicitly, because without it the privacy gain is confined to managed accounts.

A parent who does engage can do more. Through a certified household controller, the parent-managed account that Apple and Google already hold carries a plan: time budgets, windows, bedtime, rules per app, and the rules that competence has earned. The platform receives the rules, never the competence. A platform that receives a plan honours it, including where it allows more than the floor, but only where the law permits a certified household plan to lift a child above the minimum age. That condition matters. Australia’s Online Safety Amendment (Social Media Minimum Age) Act 2024 is primary legislation with no parental-consent exception, and eSafety cannot create one by guidance ([Federal Register of Legislation](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2024A00127/asmade/text)). Section 214A of the UK Act is a statutory power to set a minimum age, so the path needs the power exercised with the route written in, not a code of practice alone. The floor can be implemented everywhere now. The path requires each jurisdiction to legislate for it, and today it is impossible in Australia and would be impossible in France if its law were re-enacted unchanged. Canada’s June 2026 bill already contains an opt-out for platforms that show protective policies ([TechCrunch, July 2026](https://techcrunch.com/2026/07/21/social-media-ban-children-countries-list/)). This is that idea, made specific enough to certify.

**Switched on by a parent.** A parent or guardian switches Handrail on by choice, from the family account they already hold with Apple, Google or another vendor, and it covers only the children on that account. The same parent switches it off in the same place, with nothing to show anyone; an adult with no plan is simply an adult. Where a law sets a minimum age, certified platforms apply it to child accounts by default, so Handrail complies with a limit. It does not need one to be useful. It was designed for a two year old with a tablet, long before any ban.

### 3.2 What "good with screens" means

The standard does not define it. Parents do. But a shared vocabulary lets a parent, a guide and a study talk about the same thing. Handrail proposes six competence milestones, each paired with one or two behaviours a parent could observe. They are descriptions, not ages. They are judgements a parent records as a milestone name and a date, not events the system logs.

1. **Stopping.** Ends a session when told; then when the timer says; then by choice. Observable: stopped at the limit without a lock on most school days this month.
2. **Choosing.** Picks apps and content for a reason they can state. Observable: can say what they made this week, not only what they watched.
3. **Meeting.** Plays and talks with known people online and knows what a stranger is. Observable: can say, when asked, what they would do if someone they do not know messaged them, and who they would tell.
4. **Noticing.** Recognises a design built to hold them and can name it: autoplay, streaks, endless feed, loot boxes. Observable: can explain why a feed keeps going.
5. **Judging.** Weighs what they see. Observable: can tell an advert from a friend.
6. **Owning.** Sets their own limits and keeps them most of the time. Observable: has chosen their own bedtime for a school week and kept it.

Milestones are parental judgements that the ledger informs. They are not scores a system assigns. A child telling a parent about a worrying message is never recorded by any Handrail component, as a milestone, a count or anything else; a disclosure that is scored stops being a disclosure. The pilot in section 8 is where the inference from ledger to readiness is tested rather than assumed.

The positive evidence for this approach is the parental mediation literature, which the harm debate tends to skip. EU Kids Online’s work across Europe, and Livingstone and Helsper’s studies of active against restrictive mediation, find that restriction lowers online risk but also lowers skills and opportunities, while active, enabling mediation preserves skills and protects as children grow ([Livingstone and Helsper, 2008](https://doi.org/10.1080/08838150802437396); [Livingstone et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1111/jcom.12277); [EU Kids Online 2020](https://www.lse.ac.uk/media-and-communications/research/research-projects/eu-kids-online)). Handrail is enabling mediation written down.

### 3.3 One child’s path

Picture one child. Call her Maja.

At four, Maja has a tablet with three apps her parents chose. Everything counts. The screen shows, in pictures, how much is left.

At seven, she asks for a game. Her parents look it up in their app. The community tags say other parents mark it creative and offline-ok, and that it has in-app purchases. They allow it. Purchases need approval. It counts at half rate because it is building, not scrolling. Her ledger shows the rule and the reason in words she can read.

At ten, she wants voice chat with school friends. The community profile her parents follow shows that friends-only multiplayer is a different thing from chat with strangers, and that most families in that profile allow the first at ten and the second at thirteen. Her parents allow friends-only. They have switched on a guide in sentry mode this year. It drafts the conversation about strangers, because many parents find it hard to start. It cannot change any rule.

At thirteen, she wants a social account. Where the law lets a certified plan lift the floor, her parents can say yes. The profile they follow offers a starter policy, and the guide, since they use one, explains each line: private by default, no endless feed, no notifications after eight, an hour a day, with every rule visible to her. The platform receives this policy through the standard, applies it, and reports back what it actually did, so her parents know it landed. Where the platform cannot honour a rule, it says so, and the household sees "unsupported", not silence.

At fourteen, the guide, still in sentry mode, notices three late nights in a row. It suggests to Maja, not to her parents, that she move her own cut-off earlier. It cannot move it for her. She agrees and moves it herself. Her parents recorded the Noticing milestone in the spring, and from that point they stopped seeing which apps she uses. They see her totals against her budget, and whether each rule landed. The per-app detail is hers alone. Sleep is the one screen-related harm with consistent replication across studies ([Carter et al., JAMA Pediatrics 2016](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapediatrics.2016.2341)), so bedtime is the first rule a plan protects and the last a parent hands over.

At fifteen, her own app has shown Maja each month that she stops at her limit unprompted on most days. The raw ledger is kept for only a month, but the monthly reports show the pattern. Her parents, who see only totals, hand her the evening rule. The platform receives the change the same way.

At sixteen, there is nothing to switch off. She already runs most of her own rules. The last ones end on a date she and her parents agreed, not a date a statute chose. She chooses whether to export her milestone summary or delete it. The default is deletion, and everything else is deleted either way.

### 3.4 The parent and the guide

Many parents cannot do this alone. They do not know the games, cannot keep up with the ratings and are tired. So the standard allows a guide, an AI assistant that works for the family, and makes it optional. A household can run with none. Where a parent switches one on, it runs in one of two modes, sentry or control, set out in section 3.5. It reads only the fields the parent ticks from the controller and the shared community knowledge. It never reads content, messages, location or browsing, and sections 3.5 and 4.3 make that a conformance rule on the guide and on the controller, not a hope. A guide that asks for message content is not a Handrail guide. Where the guide runs is a conformance matter too: on the household’s own device or hub by default, and if any part runs remotely, only the ticked fields leave the home, with no retention and no training. The reference controller gathers minutes from the devices and network controls the household enrols, and it is permitted to observe only the app endpoints the plan names. It keeps no record of anything else, which is the difference between a timer and a wiretap.

One note on incentives. Every draft of this idea has used "twenty minutes for emptying the dishwasher" as its example of a grant. Paediatric guidance warns that using screens as a reward raises their value to the child ([American Academy of Pediatrics, Family Media Plan](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/media/Pages/default.aspx)). Grants stay in the standard, because parents will make them whatever the standard says, but a guide, where one is used, should flag reward-for-chores patterns and the default profiles should not model them.

### 3.5 The AI guide is optional

A household can run Handrail with no guide at all. The plan, the read-back, the child’s view and the floor all work without it. Nothing is sent to any AI service unless a parent switches a guide on, and the parent decides what it sees. The guide has three positions.

**Off.** The default. No guide is installed or enrolled. Nothing leaves the controller for any AI purpose. The plan runs anyway.

**Sentry.** The guide may observe and suggest. It never acts. It may receive only the fields the parent ticks from this list, and nothing else: minutes used against budget, per child, per day; the names of the apps in the plan and which rule applies to each; milestone names and the dates the parent set them; community tag lookups for apps the household is considering; and the plan’s own rules and reasons. It may suggest to the parent, or to the child where the parent allows it, for example an earlier bedtime after three late nights, or a note that a new game carries the loot-box tag. A suggestion is a message. It changes nothing until a person accepts it.

**Control.** Everything in sentry, plus the guide may act, but only inside limits the parent writes down in advance. The parent sets the envelope: for example, grant up to 15 minutes once a day; move today’s cut-off earlier, never later; pause a single app for the rest of the day. The guide may never loosen a bedtime, unlock a locked device, add an app to the plan, change a budget upward or change a milestone. Every action is an override with a lifetime (section 4.2), logged in the controller with the guide named as actor, and shown to the child in the same words the parent sees. The child can contest it as they can contest any override.

**Never, in any mode.** Messages or their content. Browsing history or page content. Location, beyond the guardian’s declared country already in the plan. Contacts or friend lists. The child’s name, date of birth or any identifier. Photos, audio or video. Device identifiers. The guide has no field for any of these in the standard. A guide that asks for one is not a Handrail guide.

**Where it runs.** On the household’s own device or hub by default. If any part runs remotely, only the ticked fields leave the home, with no retention beyond the request, no training on household data and no sharing with any third party. The controller shows the child, in plain words, that a guide is on, which mode it is in and which fields it receives.

**The certification rule.** A guide is certified and listed like a platform. To pass, it must: declare in a machine-readable manifest exactly which of the sentry fields it requests, and request nothing outside that manifest; refuse to start if the controller offers it a field not in the standard’s list; in control mode, accept only the envelope the parent set and return a result for every action it takes, so the controller can read back whether it stayed inside; write every action as an override with the guide as named actor; and, if remote, publish a retention and training statement that the steward can test with synthetic households. The test suite sends a guide fields it must refuse (message content, a location, a name) and fails it if it accepts them. It sends a control-mode guide an action outside its envelope (a bedtime moved later) and fails it if the controller does not reject the action. A guide that fails either test in the field, measured from certified controllers only, receives notice, a 90-day cure period and an appeal before delisting, on the same terms as a platform (section 4.7). A guide that is not listed cannot be enrolled by a certified controller.

## 4. The standard in plain words

Handrail is a vocabulary and a set of rules for exchanging screen policy between a household and a platform. Think IARC for ratings, or Matter for the smart home. It is not a product. It is the thing products must agree on.

### 4.1 Actors

- **The child.** The person the data is about. Sees every rule and every reason.
- **The guardians.** Parents or carers. Hold the only right to set and change policy.
- **The household controller.** Certified software in the home, on a phone, a hub or a router, that holds the policy and the ledger. The only place the child’s full record exists.
- **The platform.** Any operating system, console, store or service a child uses. Receives the effects of a policy, applies them, reports back.
- **The guide.** An AI assistant working for the household. Optional: a household may run with none. Where enrolled, it runs in sentry mode (observe and suggest) or control mode (act within limits the parent sets). Certified like a platform. Reads only the fields the parent ticks from what the controller holds (section 3.5).
- **The community service.** A shared database of app tags and policy profiles, holding nothing about any child.
- **The steward.** The non-profit that publishes the standard, runs the certification mark, issues controller credentials and hosts the community service.
- **The regulator.** Publishes the statutory default profile for its jurisdiction, recognises the mark, may require it, and audits the steward.

### 4.2 What is exchanged

Three objects move between home and platform. Each has a part that crosses the home boundary and a part that never does.

A **policy**: the standing rules for one child. The part the platform receives is: a daily time budget, rounded to 15 minutes; allowed windows, in 30-minute slots; a bedtime; a ceiling on content rating; an age band in the shape of the Google Play Age Signals defaults (under 13, 13 to 15, 16 to 17, 18 and over), onto which Apple’s Declared Age Range thresholds map ([Google Play Age Signals](https://developer.android.com/google/play/age-signals); [Apple](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/declaredagerange)); the guardian’s declared country, so the platform can select the applicable floor; and rules keyed to app identifiers, content ratings and app categories, with a time window on each, so a parent can say "school apps only, 08:00 to 15:00 on school days" or "education apps count, games wait until homework is done". Effects are plain words: allow always, allow, count half, count double, require approval, block. A Swedish household’s starting plan can be the Public Health Agency’s hours by age (1 to 2 hours at 6 to 12, 2 to 3 hours at 13 to 18, no screens before bed), published by the regulator as the default profile and changed by the parent as they see fit ([Regeringen, September 2024](https://regeringen.se/pressmeddelanden/2024/09/rekommendationer-om-digital-medieanvandning-for-barn-och-unga/)). A platform receives only the fields its declared capabilities can honour. Everything else stays home: milestones and their dates, progress against them, the guide’s reasoning, rules keyed to community tags, which the controller resolves to app identifiers before anything is sent, and the exact budget the household set.

An **override**: one parental decision with a lifetime. The platform receives effect, scope and expiry: "lock, this account, until 18:00". The reason ("dinner") and who made it are recorded in the controller and shown to the child there, and never cross the boundary. Every override expires, by default at the end of the day. Open-ended rules are policy changes, not overrides, so a punishment cannot quietly become permanent.

A **result**: the platform’s honest answer, read back from its own state. Applied, confirmed, partial, failed or unknown. If a platform could not do what was asked, it says so and says how it approximated.

Usage flows back as plain facts: this device, this app, these minutes. A child’s request flows back as a structured signal, app identifier plus request type (play, more time, unlock), with no free text. The home turns facts into a ledger. The platform never sees the ledger.

Identifiers are pairwise. The controller derives a different child identifier for each platform, as OpenID Connect does with pairwise subject identifiers ([OpenID](https://openid.net/specs/openid-connect-core-1_0.html#PairwiseAlg)), and sends no household identifier at all. No two platforms receive the same string, and the coarsened budget and windows mean the plan itself is not a fingerprint either.

**The statutory default profile** is a policy object in the same shape, published by each participating regulator in the registry, versioned, signed and keyed by jurisdiction. Where no regulator has published one, the platform’s existing under-age rules are the default and Handrail adds nothing.

### 4.2a What a plan can say, and what it may not block

A plan covers what as well as how long. A parent may allow or block apps and sites by identifier; block topics by name from the community vocabulary (gambling, loot boxes, dieting, violence, chat with strangers, and whatever else the family decides); block words and phrases in the child’s own languages; and copy a community profile such as "Swedish health guidance" or "educational first", then change any line. Topic and word rules are resolved inside the home controller to app and site identifiers, so a platform receives only allow and block effects, never the family’s reasons or vocabulary.

One limit on parents is deliberate and fixed by the standard. A short list stays reachable on every certified device whatever the plan says: emergency numbers (112 in Sweden), the children’s helpline (Bris, 116 111), healthcare advice (1177), the non-emergency police number (114 14), the suicide line (Mind, 90101), information on how to report abuse, and information about children’s rights. A parent may filter how these appear but cannot remove them. A certified controller refuses a rule that would block them, and the test suite checks it. Which further topics belong on the protected list, for example sexual and reproductive health information for older children, is a decision for the steward with children’s organisations at the table, and is listed among the owner decisions.

### 4.3 What a platform, a controller and a guide must do to comply

A platform must:

- Accept a Handrail policy and override for a child’s account from a controller whose credential the steward issued, verifying a signature and never a parent. The guardian is verified once, by the OS family account that already holds the age declaration, and the controller’s credential chains from it. Credentials are revocable.
- Apply the statutory default profile for the account’s jurisdiction to any child account with no household plan, selecting the jurisdiction from the plan’s declared country combined with its own existing lawful geo signals.
- Apply the plan, read back its own state, and report the result honestly. Unknown is reported as unknown, never as unlocked. Silent failure is a conformance violation.
- Report usage to the household, per app where it can, and say when it cannot.
- Carry a child’s structured request back to the household.
- Accept the plan from any certified controller, whoever built it. A parent sets the plan from a vendor’s own app or from an independent controller, and either must work with every certified platform. A platform may not require the parent to use the platform’s own app, may not refuse a plan because of who built the controller, and may not offer a certified controller fewer fields than it offers its own.
- Show the child a fixed system notice: "A household plan applies. Set by your guardian. Open your household plan for the reason." The reason, the actor and any disagreement live with the household’s controller, never on the platform’s screen.
- Use what it receives through Handrail only to apply the plan, read back, log conformance, prevent abuse and security incidents, and run child-safety features. Never for advertising, ad measurement, cross-service profiling, sale or any sharing beyond the household. Read-back logs are kept pseudonymous, without content, for at most twelve months, so the platform can defend itself in a dispute.
- Never ask for a date of birth, an identity document or a location through the Handrail channel. The only age source is the guardian’s existing OS family-account declaration, which Handrail consumes as a band. Existing lawful geo signals may be used to select the applicable floor.

A household controller must: observe the network only for the app endpoints the plan names, on an allow or deny list; retain no DNS or connection log beyond the minute counter; record per-device minutes only at Level 0; enrol a device only by explicit action visible to the child, never by passive discovery; exclude school-managed and employer-managed devices by default; and hold no platform credential. A controller that logs in as the parent and drives undocumented vendor endpoints is not certified. The reference implementation may offer such routes for a household’s own use, outside the mark, storing any credential in the OS keychain, reading only the fields the plan needs and showing the child that the route exists.

A guide is optional. Where a household enrols one, it must: declare in a manifest which of the permitted fields it requests (minutes against budget, app names and their rules, milestone names and dates, tag lookups, the plan’s rules and reasons) and request nothing else; run on the household’s device or hub by default; if any part is remote, receive only the fields the parent ticked, with no retention, no training and no third-party sharing; in sentry mode take no action; in control mode act only inside the envelope the parent set, never loosening a bedtime, unlocking a device, adding an app or raising a budget, and write every action as an override with the guide as named actor; refuse any field outside the standard’s list; and be certified and listed like a platform. The full rule and the tests are in section 3.5.

Conformance has five levels so a router and a social network can both take part. Level 0, Observer, reports usage. Level 1, Cut-off, can stop now and release later and confirm it. Level 2, Budget, accepts a daily allowance and windows and confirms them by read-back. Level 3, Interactive, adds grants, child requests and per-app rules. Level 4, Native, accepts the objects at one endpoint and emits the standard events. Nobody is at Level 4 today. No major vendor exposes any parental-control interface. Where a match exists, field names mirror the Matter Content Control cluster so a certified Matter television can join without renaming, and the conformance route is designed to sit beside ISO/IEC 27566-1, the new age-assurance framework standard ([ISO](https://www.iso.org/standard/88143.html)).

### 4.4 What stays in the home

Two tiers. The **raw ledger**: every minute, grant, lock and reason, per child, per day, written so a parent can show it to a child in full. Deleted on a schedule the household chooses, default 31 days. Zero retention is valid. The **persistent summary**: milestone names and the dates a parent set them, nothing else. No counts, no streaks, no events. Anything the guide says about a pattern is computed from the raw ledger and is not stored.

Visibility tapers as control does. Until a milestone the household sets, by default Noticing, both parents and child see per-app detail. After it, parents see totals against budget and enforcement health only, and the child alone sees per-app detail. The full policy and the guide’s reasoning stay in the controller. When a child leaves the plan, the child chooses export or deletion of the summary, the default is deletion, and every other record about them is deleted.

### 4.5 What never leaves

A child’s name. A date of birth. A location, beyond the guardian’s declared country. Credentials. Content, messages, browsing. Device identifiers. Per-app usage. Milestones and progress. Reasons and actors. Identifiers sent to a platform are unique to that platform. The only things a household may send to the community service, and only if a parent turns sharing on, are app identifiers and votes on tags, under per-vote anonymous tokens described in 4.6.

This is not rights rhetoric. It is how the standard meets the Children’s Code, whose standard 11 requires that children know when they are monitored, and General Comment 25, which discourages covertly documenting a child’s activity. Handrail documents overtly and shows the child. A platform receives a plan from a guardian, not a passport from a child. Apple’s own white paper argued that store-wide age verification would force all users to hand over "sensitive personally identifying information" for a limited set of apps ([Apple, February 2025](https://developer.apple.com/support/downloads/Helping-Protect-Kids-Online-2025.pdf)). Google’s public policy lead wrote in March 2025 that the Utah model creates new privacy risks for minors while not addressing the harms that drove lawmakers to act ([Pluribus News on Google’s blog post, March 2025](https://pluribusnews.com/news-and-events/google-pitches-age-verification-alternative/)). The two vendors that would be first to implement Handrail have already made its privacy argument.

### 4.6 How community knowledge flows

Parents know things ratings boards do not. Handrail carries a curated vocabulary of about thirty tags in five groups: value (educational, creative, reading), social (friends-only, chat-with-strangers, voice-chat), commerce (in-app purchases, loot boxes), content, and attention (endless feed, autoplay, no stopping point, offline-ok). Households vote, one vote per household per tag per app, on MusicBrainz rules. Duplicate votes are rejected without any household identifier: the steward issues one blind-signed token per app, tag and epoch to each household, so votes are unlinkable across apps and epochs, and votes travel through a relay so no address is logged. A tag shows when its net score is positive. Caution tags need evidence and three contributors. Visible tags are capped at ten per app. A developer may dispute a tag on its own product; the steward reviews the evidence and the tag is suppressed pending review. Parents publish named profiles ("strict under 10", "educational first", "Swedish health guidance") and others subscribe. The local plan always wins. Tags are resolved to app identifiers inside the household controller and never reach a platform, so no platform need ingest the dataset. Data is open with a nightly dump, so the service is not a single point of failure and never sees a child. The licence is ODbL in the draft; a permissive licence such as ODC-By is under consideration so a platform could ingest the dump if it chose to. The service starts empty, so before launch the steward curates the 200 to 500 most-used children’s apps against the vocabulary and marks those tags "curated" until household votes replace them. Where a tag has no votes the guide says so.

### 4.7 Certification

A platform runs a published, self-service test suite and submits the result, as OpenID Connect does; its own leaders credit cheap self-certification plus a public list with making Connect the most deployed identity protocol ([OpenID Foundation](https://openid.net/certification-accomplishments-and-next-steps/)). The steward lists certified implementations publicly with level, date and suite version. Suite versions are semantic: a minor version is backward compatible and never invalidates a listing; a major version gives a published 18-month migration window; a listing records the suite version it was tested against until its next annual re-test.

Field audit uses synthetic households only: the suite runs scripted test accounts against the live platform and the steward sees those traces. Platforms report field figures as counts (writes, confirmed, unknown, later found unlocked) with no identifiers or timestamps. A platform that fails the two field rules, confirming without read-back or showing unknown as unlocked, above a published threshold (for example unknown shown as unlocked on more than 1 per cent of locks over 30 days, measured from certified controllers only) receives written notice, a 90-day cure period and an independent appeal before delisting. Delisting is prospective and never voids compliance for the period the platform was listed. The list shows "notified" and "curing" states as well as "listed" and "delisted". Recognition across jurisdictions follows the IARC pattern: one certified result, accepted by every participating regulator ([IARC milestones](https://www.globalratings.com/milestones)).

## 5. Why each party gains

**The minister** keeps the sentence, the list and the fine, and adds a second sentence: "A parent-managed account on a certified platform may carry a graduated household plan." The floor covers the child nobody is helping. The path covers the child whose parents want to help. No parent-managed account needs a passport. The France challenge is narrowed to the accounts that carry neither a plan nor a band, and section 7 asks you to accept the band for those.

**The regulator** gets something auditable: a public registry, conformance levels, annual re-test, a published enforcement process, and a steward it can inspect. Ofcom’s own first age-assurance report favours layered protection across devices, app stores and operating systems ([Ofcom, July 2026](https://www.ofcom.org.uk/siteassets/resources/documents/online-safety/information-for-industry/age-assurance-report-2026/report-on-the-use-of-age-assurance.pdf)). Handrail is that layered architecture written down.

**The Treasury and the trade department** get a mutual-recognition case. Fifteen jurisdictions now name different ages, different service lists, different tests of "highly effective" and different penalties, from A$49.5 million to 6 per cent of global turnover. Every platform answers every regulator alone. One certified mark recognised across jurisdictions, as IARC is for ratings, is a competitiveness argument a finance ministry will own.

**Parents** get one place, in the app they already use or any certified alternative, to set rules for every screen, a guide that knows the games if they want one, and the loosening path they already want.

**Children** get a path with visible steps, a record they can read, a way to contest a rule, privacy from their parents that grows with them, and an end date.

**Platforms** get a shared bar and a safe harbour. "We support Handrail Level 2" is one sentence for fifteen regulators. An account that arrives with a recognised plan need not be age-verified by the platform, because the guardian’s OS family account already holds the age declaration; the OS band plus the plan substitutes for a per-service ID check wherever the regulator accepts it. The saving depends on the share of child accounts that arrive managed, and no public figure for Apple or Google family-account penetration was found; the pilot should produce one. The OS vendors are the natural first implementers: Apple’s Declared Age Range API ([Apple](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/declaredagerange)) and Google’s Play Age Signals API ([Android Developers Blog, July 2026](https://android-developers.googleblog.com/2026/07/google-play-age-signals-api-safer-experiences.html)) already carry a parent-set age band to apps, off by default. Google’s terms bar use of the signal for advertising, marketing, profiling or analytics ([Google Play Age Signals](https://developer.android.com/google/play/age-signals)); no equivalent public term was found for Apple. Handrail adds the plan that sits on top of the band. The ask is small: the channel that carries a band also carries a plan and returns a result.

## 6. Governance and funding of the steward

Standards that won vendor adoption against initial interest shared four features: a regulatory or liability tailwind that made the standard the cheaper route, as Global Privacy Control had after California’s Sephora enforcement ([IAPP, 2022](https://iapp.org/news/a/california-attorney-general-announces-first-ccpa-enforcement-action)); an open reference implementation vendors could ship rather than build, as Matter had ([Matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matter_(standard))); a certification programme that made "compliant" checkable; and someone with a reason to pay. Do Not Track and P3P lacked all four and died ([W3C](https://www.w3.org/TR/tracking-dnt/)). Full notes are in research/standards-precedent.md.

Handrail’s steward follows the pattern in three stages.

**Stage one, 2027.** A W3C Community Group, free to start and join, with a neutral IPR policy, so a platform engineer can participate without asking legal for a budget ([W3C](https://www.w3.org/community/about/design-goals/)). Specification, schemas, conformance suite and reference controller published under open licences. Running cost about 300,000 EUR a year, met by a named government pilot sponsor and one or two foundation sponsors.

**Stage two, 2028.** Once two independent implementations conform, a non-profit steward incorporated in Europe on the ISRG model, which runs Let’s Encrypt and two other projects on about 7 million USD a year from sponsors and small donors ([ISRG](https://www.abetterinternet.org/annual-reports/)). The open-source reference controller counts as the first implementation; the first certified platform is the second. Running cost rises to about 600,000 EUR a year with the registry, audit and credential issuance, still under a tenth of ISRG. If the gate is not reached, the specification stays open in the Community Group.

**Stage three, 2029 onward.** Membership dues on the Connectivity Standards Alliance scale, 7,500 to 112,500 USD a year by tier ([CSA](https://csa-iot.org/become-member/)), with a permanent free tier for non-profits, academics and small developers, and certification fees of about 2,000 EUR per product. The arithmetic is slow: two members at the middle tier plus two certifications is under 50,000 USD a year. Dues cover 600,000 EUR only at roughly 25 members across tiers, which on this roadmap is 2031 at the earliest. A government and sponsor line stays through stage three until that member count exists.

Principles. A permanent child-rights seat and a permanent privacy seat on the board. Platform seats capped at half. The founding company holds at most one non-voting seat, its own products are certified by the same self-service suite and listed with the same disclosure as everyone else, and the community service’s code and data are mirrored outside the company from day one. The steward never holds per-child data, never charges parents or children, and never sells data.

Honest weakness: Signal’s foundation ran an 8.6 million USD deficit in 2024 on 29 million USD revenue ([ProPublica](https://projects.propublica.org/nonprofits/organizations/824506840)). Donation funding is fragile. Who pays before certification is a government, or nobody. Section 7 makes that a costed request rather than a confession.

## 7. What we ask, costed, and what you get for it

Sweden’s inquiry delivers its final report, on age verification, on 14 November 2026, and the proposed law would start on 1 January 2028 ([Regeringen, remiss page](https://www.regeringen.se/remisser/2026/06/remiss-sou-202635-en-aldersgrans-for-barns-tillgang-till-sociala-medier/)). No bill can come before a new government is in place. The window between the final report and the bill is when this is most useful to the officials who will draft it. The UK ban is pencilled for spring 2027. The first certified platform cannot realistically exist before 2028. Nothing here asks any government to wait or to repeal. Four asks fit the calendar. The first three work in any country legislating a minimum age; the fourth is Sweden’s to make in Brussels.

1. **A future compliance route in secondary legislation now, with a safe harbour.** One clause: a parent-managed account governed by a certified graduated household plan, at a conformance level the regulator names, may be treated as meeting the minimum-age duty. A platform that applies a plan received from a steward-certified controller, and reads back honestly, has discharged that duty for that account; its other duties under the Act are unchanged. The clause also accepts the OS family account’s parent-declared age band as a lawful age signal. It commits you to nothing until a mark exists. It gives platforms a reason to build toward it. In Sweden the clause belongs in the bill that follows SOU 2026:35, which today has no parental route of any kind. The clause does not reopen the age decision, which the inquiry deliberately kept away from parents; it governs what a parent controls above the floor, and how a platform shows it has done its part. Where the minimum age already sits in primary legislation with no parental route, as in Australia, the same words need an amendment.
2. **A named pilot sponsorship.** Australia’s trial of 60 technologies from 48 vendors was funded at A$6.5 million ([Australian Government, trial findings](https://minister.infrastructure.gov.au/wells/media-release/albanese-government-releases-age-assurance-technology-trial-findings); [Department of Social Services](https://www.dss.gov.au/national-plan-end-violence-against-women-and-children/progress/first-action-plan-progress/first-action-plan-activities-addendum/age-assurance-technologies-protect-children-harmful-online-content)). A sum of that order, about 3.5 million EUR over two years, funds the following, with the pilot run in Swedish households and evaluated by a Swedish university.

   | Item | Estimate |
   |---|---|
   | Steward, two years at 300,000 then 600,000 EUR | 900,000 EUR |
   | Reference controller and conformance suite, open source | 800,000 EUR |
   | Pilot, 400 households at about 2,000 EUR each (devices, support, incentives) | 800,000 EUR |
   | Independent, pre-registered evaluation by a university partner | 700,000 EUR |
   | Contingency | 300,000 EUR |
   | **Total** | **3.5 million EUR** |

   These are planning figures, not quotes. Pilot data is held by the university as data controller, with separate consent for each child and age-appropriate assent, no link between pilot records and the community service, and deletion at study end.
3. **A published default plan and a twelve-month review point.** Ask the Public Health Agency (Folkhälsomyndigheten) to publish its hours by age as the Swedish default household plan in the registry, so every certified controller, whoever built it, starts from the same national guidance, and ask Mediemyndigheten to name the mark in its guidance to parents. Write a review point into the ban’s own commencement, at which the regulator reports on the pilot’s outcomes beside the ban’s.
4. **One line to Brussels before 3 December 2026.** The Commission’s KIDS Act proposal gives 13 and 14 year olds a parent-controlled account with a daily limit and approved contacts, and requires member states to provide a free way to prove parental responsibility ([Commission, KIDS Act FAQ](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/faqs/kids-act-explained)). Sweden’s feedback, and its position in Council, should ask that this account can be run through any certified household plan, from any vendor, so the tier is not built five times in five incompatible ways. That is the same ask as the clause, made at the level where platform duties will actually be set.

What you get for it is the evidence nobody has. Of the 40 restriction experiments reviewed by Lind, Schueller and Odgers, none reported a mean participant age under 18 ([Lind, Schueller and Odgers, May 2026](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fdpys.2026.1805989/full)). The pilot produces consented, minimal, longitudinal data on how children acquire screen competence, published whether or not it flatters the idea.

## 8. Three-year roadmap

**2027: the standard and the pilot.** Handrail 1.0 published as a Community Group draft with schemas, conformance rules and the tag vocabulary, with the Swedish default profile (the Public Health Agency’s hours by age) as the first worked example. Reference household controller released as open source, working over the device controls and allow-listed network controls that households already own. Advisory groups formed: child rights, privacy, parents, platforms, researchers from both camps. Government pilot sponsor named. Pilot design pre-registered with a university: outcome measures are sleep, self-reported wellbeing, media literacy, parent-child conflict and the six milestones, not account counts or minutes. The steward’s curation of the first 200 to 500 apps against the vocabulary begins. Briefings to Socialdepartementet, Mediemyndigheten and Folkhälsomyndigheten in Sweden, then Ofcom, eSafety and the Commission’s DSA minors team, each asked the same question: if a platform certified at Level 2, would you say so in guidance?

**2028: the first platform and the registry.** Conformance suite and public registry live. One OS vendor or console maker at Level 2, most likely one already carrying a parent-set age band or already in Matter. Community service live with curated tags, seeded with IARC age ratings and content descriptors, which do not supply the attention or social tags; those are curated. Steward incorporated in the fourth quarter, the reference controller and the first certified platform being the two conforming implementations. First pilot results published.

**2029: the regulator cites the mark.** One regulator, anywhere, names Handrail conformance in guidance, a code of practice or a procurement document. A second platform certified. Membership opens. First outcome report against the ban baseline.

If 2029 does not deliver a regulatory cite, the honest position is that the standard remains useful to the households that run it, and the specification stays open for whoever picks it up next.

## 9. Risks and objections, with answers

**"Parents want a ban."** Around nine in ten of the parents whose consultation answers were broken out did. Handrail does not take it from them. The floor stays. The path is for the parents who want more, and the default profile covers the children whose parents do not engage.

**"Platforms never adopt open standards voluntarily."** Correct. No vendor exposed an age signal until Texas and Utah made them. Handrail does not ask for goodwill. It rides the laws that already force parent-mediated channels and asks the regulator to name the mark. Until then the reference controller works over the household’s own devices and allow-listed network controls, under the observation rules in 4.3, without anyone’s permission.

**"Apple and Google already have age-range APIs, and could ship this themselves."** They are the foundation, not the competitor, and they could. Screen Time and Family Link are household controllers already. What neither can be is neutral: Apple will never control a PlayStation or a Windows PC, and no household is single-vendor. If one OS vendor ships a proprietary equivalent, a regulator still needs a mark that is not owned by one of the regulated, and the households still need the cross-vendor layer. The open specification and the open data are not what holds this together. Neutrality and the households are.

**"A parent signal can be faked."** So can a ban, and Australia shows how often. Handrail’s floor is no weaker than the ban’s; its path serves the willing majority. The pilot measures how many route around a plan they helped write.

**"The AI guide is surveillance."** It would be if it were compulsory, if it read content, or if it ran in a vendor’s cloud on the whole ledger. It is none of these. A household can run with no guide, and nothing is sent anywhere unless a parent switches one on. In sentry mode it sees only the fields the parent ticks and can only suggest. In control mode it acts only inside limits the parent wrote down, and every action is shown to the child. The standard gives it no field for content, messages or location, requires it to run in the home by default, and certifies it against those limits (section 3.5). The ledger is visible to the child by design, which is what the Children’s Code requires and what General Comment 25 asks for, and from Noticing the per-app detail is the child’s alone. The weakness is real: a conformance mark constrains the standard, not what a vendor does beside it.

**"Competence milestones are a middle-class fantasy."** The strongest objection. Default profiles, a guide that does the thinking, and community profiles from households like one’s own are the answers. They may not be enough. The pilot must include families across income, education and shared-device households, and the data must be published either way.

**"The competence tier is a proxy dressed as a measure."** Agreed, if it were a measure. It is a parental judgement the ledger informs, held only in the home. The pilot tests whether ledger patterns predict anything a researcher would accept.

**"Anonymous aggregate insight is still child data."** Agreed, so the steward publishes none. The registry shows platform and certification data only. The only longitudinal child data is the pilot’s, held by the university under its own ethics approval, and it is never joined to the community service.

**"The evidence is too early to judge Australia."** Agreed. This paper says the early evidence is weak, not that the policy has failed. The review point in section 7 exists to judge both instruments on the same evidence.

**Weaknesses in our own evidence.** Several primary pages (eSafety, Ofcom, the BMJ, the Commission press corner, Apple’s newsroom, Google’s policy blog, Roblox’s help centre) blocked automated fetching, so figures were cross-checked against two secondary reports each and must be re-read from source before publication. The eSafety three-month figures in section 2.5 come via Engadget and the Ofcom 2022 figures via LBC. Sweden’s signature on the Jutland Declaration is inferred from the list of non-signatories. The claim that the KIDS Act bars higher national ages rests on one law-firm reading and must be checked against the text. The daily-use figures from Ungar & medier 2025 are taken from the government’s June 2026 press release, not the report. Ministerial ownership in section 1a may change within weeks because government formation is unresolved. The BMJ sample is 408 children in one state. VPN figures are vendor-reported. The parental mediation and sleep citations in section 3 were added in this synthesis and should be checked against the papers. The cost table in section 7 is a planning estimate. Leading academic critics of the harm thesis have industry advisory ties, which Appendix A discloses.

## 10. Call to action

To a minister: keep the floor. Add one clause for the path and its safe harbour, publish the default plan, sponsor the pilot, set the review date. In Sweden, do this between the final report on 14 November and the bill, and say the same in Brussels before 3 December.

To a regulator: tell us what a certified household-plan layer would need to look like for you to cite it, and publish your default profile in the registry. The conformance suite will be built to that.

To an OS vendor: the channel that carries an age band can carry a plan. Nominate one engineer to the Community Group.

To researchers and child-rights organisations: the under-18 evidence does not exist. Help design the instrument that produces it.

To parents: when your government offers you a ban, ask what happens at fifteen, and ask what happens the day your child borrows your account.

The ban is what you build when you cannot picture anything else. The diagrams and screens that accompany this paper picture it.

## Appendix A. The harm evidence, both sides

Kept out of the body because it is politically unusable and scientifically contested. The small-effects position: Orben and Przybylski found technology use explains at most 0.4 per cent of variance in adolescent wellbeing ([Nature Human Behaviour, 2019](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-018-0506-1)); Odgers' review found a mix of no, small and mixed associations, with evidence that struggling young people use platforms more rather than platforms causing the struggle ([Nature, 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-024-00902-2)); Lind, Schueller and Odgers reviewed 40 restriction studies and found none with a mean participant age under 18, and disclose that Odgers advises YouTube and Schueller advises Headspace ([Frontiers, 2026](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fdpys.2026.1805989/full)). The harm position: Braghieri, Levy and Makarin used Facebook’s staggered college rollout to find a causal effect on mental health ([AER, 2022](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.20211218)); Allcott and colleagues' deactivation experiment found modest wellbeing gains from a month off Facebook ([AER, 2020](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.20190658)); Haidt’s The Anxious Generation (2024) argues for a phone-based childhood as a cause of the adolescent mental-health decline. The one finding with consistent replication across camps is sleep displacement from bedtime device use ([Carter et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapediatrics.2016.2341)), which is why bedtime is the rule Handrail protects first.

## Appendix B. Relationship to the technical draft

A phase-1 technical specification (Handrail 0.1: policies per child, overrides, tag-weighted usage, community tags, privacy rules) and an honest platform matrix showing that no vendor exposes a parental-control API exist as working documents. They are household-scale. The platform-facing Level 4 interface, the statutory default profile, the guide’s data boundary, the controller observation rules and the certification mark are proposed here and not yet specified. No demo is offered because a half-working demonstration of what today’s platforms permit would show less than this paper describes, and would be judged on what it cannot do.

## Appendix C. For funders

Kept out of the policy paper by design. The capital flows, competitive map, Swedish grant route, company structure, market sizing and exit analysis belong in FUNDING-LANDSCAPE.md, which is not revised in this draft. The short version, corrected. The steward, not any company, operates the standard’s layer: the mark, the registry, the credentials and the community service. The company builds and sells a certified household controller and guide, as a paid household subscription from day one in 2027, working over the household’s own devices and network before any platform adopts anything, and holding no copy of any ledger, which lives in the home. The steward’s rule against charging parents binds the steward, not the company. The company’s moat is not the open specification or the open data, which anyone may take. It is cross-vendor neutrality, the guide’s quality, and the installed base of households. The regulatory cite in 2029 widens that moat; it is not the precondition for revenue. Inference cost for a guide that runs partly remote is the main variable cost and must appear in the commercial model. The standard, not the company, is what the regulator cites.
