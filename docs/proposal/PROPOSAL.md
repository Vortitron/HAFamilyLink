# Handrail: a floor and a path

22 October 2026

**Parents in control. One plan covers all their child’s screens.**

Handrail is a proposed open standard, with a certification mark, for children’s screen time. It is not an app. It is three things: a written plan for each child; one shared way of writing that plan, which every certified device and service can read; and a mark that shows a product really follows it. The law sets a minimum age, and that stays as the floor. Above that floor, the family’s own plan is the path: a parent writes each child’s plan once, in the parental app the family already uses or in any certified alternative. Every certified device, console, app and platform follows that plan and reports back what it actually did. The child sees every rule and the reason for it.

Draft 0.5, 22 October 2026. Written for ministers, officials and regulators in Sweden, where this will be proposed first, and for anyone else about to legislate a minimum age for social media or screens. It is a design, not a product. It contains no code and no demo. No author is named in this draft.

## Summary

**What a parent gets.** Handrail is for every screen a child uses, from a first tablet at two to a first phone, not only for social media. Set the rules once. Two hours a day, and it means two hours in total across the tablet, the console and the phone, not two hours on each. School apps only on a school day until three o’clock. Or homework time, where education apps count and games wait. Bedtime at nine, phone out of the bedroom. Then the plan holds on every screen in the house, whichever company made the screen. Apple keeps Screen Time, Google keeps Family Link, Sony keeps its app. Once their apps are certified, any one of these apps can set the plan for the whole household and show what the other companies’ devices actually did, and a family that prefers an independent app, website or home hub can use that instead. The child sees the same rules in the same words, with the reason beside each one. A plan says how long, what and when: which apps and sites are allowed, which sites, topics and words are blocked, and when each applies. A parent or guardian chooses to switch Handrail on, using the family account they already have, and can switch it off the same way.

**Why today does not work.** A parent of two runs four or five separate parental apps today. None of these apps can see what the others do. Family Link counts time on each device separately. Limits set on an iPhone do not reach a Switch or a PlayStation. Of eleven vendor tools checked for this paper, nine can set a daily limit, but none can see another company’s devices or let a parent link the tools together. In Sweden, 92 per cent of parents have heard of the Public Health Agency’s recommendations, yet 85 per cent worry they cannot protect their child online and one in five do not know how ([Internetstiftelsen, December 2025](https://internetstiftelsen.se/om-oss/press/pressmeddelanden/svenska-foraldrar-oroar-sig-for-att-inte-kunna-skydda-sitt-barn-pa-natet/)). Knowing the limit and applying it across five apps are different things.

**Why a ban alone is not the answer.** Sweden’s inquiry, SOU 2026:35, proposes that no child holds a social media account before the year they turn 15, from 1 January 2028 ([Regeringen, June 2026](https://www.regeringen.se/rattsliga-dokument/statens-offentliga-utredningar/2026/06/sou-202635/)). This paper does not ask Sweden to drop that. It asks Sweden to look at what a bare ban does. Australia has run the experiment. Three months in, 81 per cent of 10 to 15 year olds still used a platform covered by the ban, down from 86 per cent before; of those who still held an account, about one in nine said a parent had helped them keep it ([eSafety, August 2026](https://www.esafety.gov.au/sites/default/files/2026-08/Early-days-Early-Insights-SMMA-Evaluation-Understanding-experiences-of-social-media-age-restrictions-August2026.pdf)). The commonest way round the ban at home is a parent’s adult account or a new account with an adult birthday, and at that moment every teen protection the platform built disappears: private by default, strangers cannot message, sleep mode, time reminders. A ban does not remove the child. It removes the protection. It also makes every adult prove their age, pushes children to apps the ban does not cover, and turns enforcement into a chase that never ends. The Chancellor of Justice, Bris, the Children’s Ombudsman and the children the inquiry heard all said the same thing in the consultation: a limit alone is not enough, and the inquiry did not weigh alternatives.

**What Handrail adds.** Keep the legal minimum age as the floor. Where the law sets an age, certified platforms apply it to child accounts as the law requires, and Handrail adds no age check of its own. Above the floor, a parent-managed account follows the family’s own plan. The plan loosens step by step as the child shows they can stop, choose and judge. Platforms receive only the parts of the plan they need. Each one knows the child only by a code, and no two platforms get the same code. They never receive a name, a date of birth, a document, messages or location. The child’s record stays with the controller the family chose, under the family’s own account, and is deleted after 31 days by default. An AI guide is optional. A household can run with no guide at all. Where a parent switches one on, it runs in sentry mode (sees only what the parent ticks, suggests, never acts) or control mode (acts only within limits the parent sets, every action shown to the child). In either mode, it never sees messages, browsing, location or the child’s name.

**What we ask of Sweden.** Four things, all possible whichever government forms. One clause in the bill that follows the inquiry, recognising certified household plans and naming who may certify them. It adds no duty on platforms, because the inquiry found Sweden cannot add duties under the DSA. Protection for platforms that apply a plan honestly (a ‘safe harbour’) belongs in the EU law. Ask the Public Health Agency to publish its hours by age as the default Swedish plan. Sponsor a pilot of 400 Swedish households with an independent evaluation, published whatever it finds, at about 3.5 million EUR for the whole programme. And before feedback closes on 3 December 2026 on the KIDS Act, the European Commission’s proposed EU law, ask that a certified open standard counts as meeting its rule that the largest platforms’ parental tools work with other companies’ tools, that platforms applying it honestly are protected, and that its parent-controlled account for 13 and 14 year olds can be managed with any certified plan.

**The bet.** If Sweden builds proper parental controls before it has to enforce a ban, it may never need to fight its own children over one. The EU may still push for a ban. That is the next fight, and Sweden would argue it from evidence rather than from hope.

## Sweden, where this is proposed first

Sweden is further on than most countries. It has national guidance, a proposed floor, a school phone ban, and a consultation that has just asked for something beside a ban. What it lacks is the tool that lets a parent apply any of it across the screens a child actually uses.

**The guidance.** On 2 September 2024 the Public Health Agency (Folkhälsomyndigheten) published screen time recommendations by age: no screens under 2, at most 1 hour a day at 2 to 5, 1 to 2 hours at 6 to 12, 2 to 3 hours at 13 to 18, no screens before bed and phones out of the bedroom at night. It also advised waiting until 13 before a child gets a smartphone ([Regeringen, September 2024](https://regeringen.se/pressmeddelanden/2024/09/rekommendationer-om-digital-medieanvandning-for-barn-och-unga/)). On 1 June 2026 the agency added advice for parents’ own habits: put the phone away when you are with your child, agree phone-free zones at home, think before posting pictures of your child ([Folkhälsomyndigheten, June 2026](https://www.folkhalsomyndigheten.se/nyheter-och-press/nyhetsarkiv/2026/juni/folkhalsomyndigheten-presenterar-nya-rekommendationer-om-foraldrars-skarmvanor/)). The guidance has moved behaviour. Mediemyndigheten’s Ungar & medier 2025 found that 9 to 12 year olds spend 40 minutes a day less on digital devices than in 2022, and that the share of 9 year olds with no mobile at all has nearly doubled ([Mediemyndigheten via TT, September 2025](https://via.tt.se/pressmeddelande/4064171/ny-statistik-anvandningen-av-digitala-medier-minskar-bland-yngre-barn?publisherId=3235838&lang=sv)). Mediemyndigheten links the fall partly to the recommendations and the public debate. Guidance plus parents already works. The problem is that parents cannot apply it across vendors. Internetstiftelsen found 92 per cent of parents have heard of the recommendations, 39 per cent say they changed nothing, 85 per cent worry they cannot protect their child online and one in five do not know how. Rows about screen time peak in mellanstadiet (ages 10 to 12), where 58 per cent of parents report them ([Internetstiftelsen, December 2025](https://internetstiftelsen.se/om-oss/press/pressmeddelanden/svenska-foraldrar-oroar-sig-for-att-inte-kunna-skydda-sitt-barn-pa-natet/)). A Swedish household plan can start from the Public Health Agency’s hours by age and let the parent change what they like.

**The inquiry.** On 9 October 2025 the government appointed an inquiry (Dir. 2025:91) led by Lisa Englund Krafft to analyse an age limit for social media and to follow the EU’s age verification work ([Riksdagen, Dir. 2025:91](https://www.riksdagen.se/sv/dokument-och-lagar/dokument/kommittedirektiv/en-aldersgrans-till-skydd-for-barns-halsa-och_hdb191/)). Its interim report, SOU 2026:35 of 2 June 2026, proposes a law barring logged-in social media accounts before the calendar year a child turns 15, in force 1 January 2028, with platforms responsible for checking age under the EU’s Digital Services Act (DSA). Parents cannot choose to let a younger child have an account, because the inquiry judged that parental consent would make protection unequal and expose parents to peer pressure. There are no penalties for children or parents, because children must dare to tell adults what happened ([Regeringen, SOU 2026:35](https://www.regeringen.se/rattsliga-dokument/statens-offentliga-utredningar/2026/06/sou-202635/)). The inquiry’s own text records that platforms do not check age at sign-up and that existing parental controls are limited and depend on linking a teen account to a parent account. It also records what the children it heard said: a limit is easy to get round, will not be followed, and will move children to less regulated platforms, and parents and school should give children support and tools instead ([SOU 2026:35, pp 67 to 69](https://www.regeringen.se/contentassets/d1b21d5210774bfba9797e08883c9633/sou-2026_35_pdf-a_webb.pdf)). Age verification is left to the final report, due 14 November 2026 ([Regeringen, remiss page](https://www.regeringen.se/remisser/2026/06/remiss-sou-202635-en-aldersgrans-for-barns-tillgang-till-sociala-medier/)). Separately, a national mobile phone ban in compulsory schools took effect on 1 August 2026: phones are collected at the start of the school day and handed back at the end ([Skolverket, June 2026](https://www.skolverket.se/om-skolverket/nyheter-och-pressmeddelanden/nyheter/nyheter/2026-06-17-flera-regelandringar-for-trygghet-sakerhet-och-studiero-borjar-galla-i-sommar)).

**The consultation.** The public consultation (remiss) on SOU 2026:35 closed on 16 October 2026. Read together, the responses ask for something beside a ban. The Chancellor of Justice (Justitiekanslern) advised against the proposal, calling it a significant intrusion into children’s fundamental rights and criticising the inquiry for proposing a limit without any closer consideration of alternatives ([Dagens Juridik, October 2026](https://www.dagensjuridik.se/nyheter/jk-sager-nej-till-aldersgrans-pa-sociala-medier/)). Bris neither supports nor opposes the limit, says a ban alone will not keep children safe, warns that some children will look for ways round it or move to less safe places, and says the main responsibility now lies with the platforms ([Bris via TT, October 2026](https://via.tt.se/pressmeddelande/4598937/bris-aldersgrans-pa-sociala-medier-racker-inte?publisherId=1239792&lang=sv)). The Children’s Ombudsman (Barnombudsmannen) backs a limit in principle but says it can never answer all the risks and must be paired with support to children and parents ([Barnombudsmannen, September 2026](https://www.regeringen.se/contentassets/9749352ad9724cd89625ad26807ea933/barnombudsmannen.pdf)). Internetstiftelsen rejects the proposal in its current form, citing children getting round the ban, children moving to less regulated services and the privacy cost of making every adult prove their age ([Internetstiftelsen, October 2026](https://www.regeringen.se/contentassets/9749352ad9724cd89625ad26807ea933/internetstiftelsen.pdf)). Mediemyndigheten accepts a limit but says it cannot alone handle the risks and must be combined with support to parents and school. Researchers and mental health groups add that a ban hits vulnerable young people hardest ([NSPH, October 2026](https://nsph.se/2026/10/15/aldersgrans-for-sociala-medier-far-inte-sla-hardast-mot-de-mest-sarbara/)). None of these bodies is against protecting children. They are asking what sits beside the floor.

**Who owns it.** The age limit and the Public Health Agency’s work sit with the Minister for Social Affairs, Jakob Forssmed (KD), who commissioned both. The school phone ban sits with the Minister for Education, Simona Mohamsson (L). The state e-ID, available from age 9 from 1 December 2026 and the basis for Sweden’s EU digital identity wallet, sits with the Minister for Civil Affairs, Erik Slottner (KD) ([Regeringen, December 2025](https://www.regeringen.se/pressmeddelanden/2025/12/expertgrupp-utsedd-till-utredningen-om-en-aldersgrans-pa-sociala-medier/)). The EU proposal, the KIDS Act, is handled by the Ministry of Culture, which plans to consult stakeholders in November 2026 ([Government fact memorandum 2026/27:FPM10, October 2026](https://data.riksdagen.se/dokument/HE06FPM10)). Under the DSA, Sweden’s coordinator is the Post and Telecom Authority, PTS, with Mediemyndigheten and Konsumentverket as the other two competent authorities ([PTS, February 2024](https://pts.se/nyheter-och-pressmeddelanden/pts-samordnare-och-behorig-myndighet-for-dsa-forordningen/)). As of 22 October 2026 no new government has been formed after the 13 September election, and the outgoing ministers remain in office as caretakers ([Riksdagen, 15 October 2026](https://www.riksdagen.se/sv/aktuellt/aktuelltnotiser/2026/okt/15/nej-till-magdalena-andersson-s-som-statsminister_cmsd2ab1134-a03c-4be2-b345-af0aacff33d3sv/)). The limit has cross-party support, including from the Social Democrats, who proposed their own strict 15 limit in October 2025. Whichever government forms, a 15 limit is likely to proceed. This paper is written for the officials in Socialdepartementet who will draft the bill, and for whoever holds the portfolio when they do.

**The EU pressure.** Sweden has leaned into EU action. It offered experts to the Commission’s child safety panel in October 2025 ([Regeringen, October 2025](https://www.regeringen.se/pressmeddelanden/2025/10/sverige-vill-bidra-med-experter-till-eus-utredningar-av-sociala-medier/)) and was among the 25 EU states that signed the Jutland Declaration calling for EU-level age verification (inferred: only Estonia and Belgium declined) ([Heise, October 2025](https://www.heise.de/en/news/EU-states-favour-social-media-ban-for-children-few-dissenters-10750803.html)). On 17 September 2026 the Commission proposed the EU KIDS Act: no accounts under 13, a parent-controlled account at 13 and 14 with a one-hour daily limit and approved contacts, an own account from 15, certified age verification, and fines up to 6 per cent of turnover ([Commission, KIDS Act FAQ](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/faqs/kids-act-explained)). One law-firm reading of the text says member states could not set a higher age than the EU does; this has not been checked against the legal text, and it conflicts with the Commission panel’s July report ([DWT, September 2026](https://www.dwt.com/blogs/privacy--security-law-blog/2026/09/eu-kids-act-online-child-safety-proposal)). In July 2026 the Commission objected, according to press reports, to France setting up its own supervision on top of the DSA, and France’s constitutional court later struck the French law down ([AFP via The Star, July 2026](https://www.thestar.com.my/tech/tech-news/2026/07/07/eu-tells-france-to-amend-social-media-ban-law)). The lesson for Sweden is that a national age law must not repeat the DSA’s duties, and must leave platform duties to the EU. The EU text already points Handrail’s way. The Commission’s 2025 guidelines on minors ask for tools for guardians that work across devices ([Commission, July 2025](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/library/commission-publishes-guidelines-protection-minors)), and the KIDS Act proposal would require the largest platforms’ tools for guardians to work with other companies’ tools ([COM(2026) 681, Article 20(5)](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:52026PC0681)). Feedback on the KIDS Act is open until 3 December 2026 ([Commission, October 2026](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/faqs/kids-act-explained)).

**Why doing it properly first matters.** The KIDS Act’s parent-controlled account for 13 and 14 year olds, with a daily limit and approved contacts set by a guardian, is a household plan by another name. Nobody has said how it works across vendors. Sweden has national hours by age, a measured fall in younger children’s screen time, a proposed floor, and a consultation asking for alternatives. It can show how a parent-controlled account works across Apple, Google, Sony, Nintendo, Microsoft and the social media platforms before the EU fixes the rules. If proper parental controls arrive first, and the evidence shows families using them, Sweden may never need to enforce a ban against its own children. The honest caveat: the EU may still legislate a ban, and under the DSA Sweden could not stop it. That is the next fight. A working alternative with Swedish evidence behind it is the strongest position to argue from, and the only one that does not depend on hoping the chase will one day end.

## What a ban costs, on the evidence

The evidence is young, most of it measured three months after the ban began, and it already points one way. Removing an account does not remove the child.

&#91;embedded content: two instruments side by side · a ban and a household plan\]

A ban and Handrail are both ways to make the rules work. A ban checks everyone and teaches nothing. Handrail checks no child’s identity and teaches for ten years. Handrail is offered beside the ban, not instead of it.

**Bans leak.** Australia’s under-16 law came into force on 10 December 2025. Platforms removed or restricted 4.7 million accounts in the first half of December, a count of accounts rather than children ([eSafety, January 2026](https://esafety.gov.au/newsroom/media-releases/platforms-restrict-access-to-47-million-under-16-accounts-across-australia)). In July 2026 eSafety published a survey of more than 4,000 children and families, measured in March: over 81 per cent of 10 to 15 year olds were still using at least one restricted platform, down from about 86 per cent before the law; account ownership from 52 to 42 per cent ([Al Jazeera, August 2026](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/8/1/most-australian-teens-still-online-three-months-after-under-16-ban)). Around half of the children who kept accounts said the platform never checked their age. A BMJ study of 408 children in New South Wales found over 85 per cent still using covered platforms, almost a fifth through a fake account, and reported insufficient evidence of any early substantial effect ([The National, June 2026](https://www.thenationalnews.com/future/technology/2026/06/25/age-verification-flaws-undermine-australias-social-media-ban-for-under-16s/)).

**Bans verify adults.** A minimum age can only be enforced by checking everyone. Ofcom counted over 69 million age checks on 32 UK services in the second half of 2025 ([Ofcom, July 2026](https://www.ofcom.org.uk/siteassets/resources/documents/online-safety/information-for-industry/age-assurance-report-2026/report-on-the-use-of-age-assurance.pdf)). France’s Conseil constitutionnel struck down the under-15 law on 14 August 2026 as a disproportionate limit on freedom of expression that ignored age and maturity, gave parents no way to lift it, and forced every user to prove their age with no legal limits ([LCP, August 2026](https://lcp.fr/actualites/reseaux-sociaux-pourquoi-le-conseil-constitutionnel-a-censure-l-interdiction-pour-les)). A US federal judge compared Texas’s app-store age law to making every bookshop check every customer at the door ([9to5Mac, December 2025](https://9to5mac.com/2025/12/23/texas-judge-blocks-state-app-store-age-verification-law/)). About 850 million people worldwide have no ID at all ([EFF, October 2026](https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2026/10/when-no-id-means-no-internet-age-verification-and-right-access-information)).

**The data leaks.** A Discord support vendor exposed about 70,000 government ID photos collected for age-verification appeals, by Discord’s count; the attackers claimed far more ([The Record, October 2025](https://therecord.media/discord-government-docs-exposed-breach)). The Tea app leaked about 13,000 verification selfies it had promised to delete ([The Record, July 2025](https://therecord.media/tea-app-data-breach-stolen-ids-leaked)). Australia’s own technology trial found vendors retaining data in "over-anticipation" of regulators’ needs, and face-scan age estimates wrongly rejecting 8.5 per cent of 16 year olds at an age check set at 16. Some systems were less accurate for non-Caucasian and female-presenting users near the cut-off, and Indigenous people were under-represented in the data used to train them ([Australian Government, August 2025](https://www.infrastructure.gov.au/department/media/publications/age-assurance-technology-trial-final-report)).

**People find ways round the checks.** When UK age checks went live in July 2025, Proton VPN (an app that hides which country a user is in) reported UK sign-ups up more than 1,400 per cent within hours ([The Register, July 2025](https://www.theregister.com/2025/07/28/uk_vpn_demand_soars/)). When Australia’s adult-site checks began on 9 March 2026, Proton VPN was already 19th among free iPhone apps in Australia, by the count of the app-market firm Sensor Tower ([The Guardian via Daily Mirror, March 2026](https://www.dailymirror.lk/international/Australians-turn-to-VPNs-after-adult-website-blocked-under-new-age-laws/107-334841)). The UK figure comes from Proton itself. Both show the trend, not its exact size.

**Workarounds remove the protections.** Not every hole in a ban is made by a clever teenager. Parents make many of them, because their child’s friends are all there and because nobody is punished for it. Australia’s eSafety evaluation three months in found that, of children who still held an account three months in, about half said the platform had not yet asked their age, just over a third had an account listing them as 16 or over, about one in nine said a parent had helped them, and 7.4 per cent had used a VPN ([eSafety, August 2026](https://www.esafety.gov.au/sites/default/files/2026-08/Early-days-Early-Insights-SMMA-Evaluation-Understanding-experiences-of-social-media-age-restrictions-August2026.pdf)). Parents were helping even before the law took effect: a 13 year old told Crikey, "I just put my mum’s face in for the facial verification and it worked" ([Crikey, December 2025](https://www.crikey.com.au/?p=1230975)). Four months in, 61 per cent of 12 to 15 year olds who had accounts before still had one, 70 per cent of them called getting round the ban easy, and 7 to 10 per cent had a friend or family member set up a new over-16 account for them ([Molly Rose Foundation, April 2026](https://mollyrosefoundation.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/MRF_Australia-Social-Media-Ban-Research_Briefing-April-26.pdf)). In the UK, 39 per cent of 11 to 17 year olds have beaten an age check; 11 per cent of those who posed as older used a parent’s or relative’s ID; and of children who use a VPN, 22 per cent were helped by a parent to set it up and 43 per cent have a parent paying for it ([DSIT and BMG Research, July 2026](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/childrens-circumvention-behaviours-online)).

**What the child loses.** This is the danger nobody says out loud. The moment a child moves onto a parent’s account, or a new account with an adult birthday, every protection the platform built for teenagers disappears. An Instagram teen account is private by default, can be messaged only by people the teen already follows, carries a sensitive content filter, a 60-minute daily reminder and a sleep mode from 22:00 to 07:00, and a 13 to 15 year old needs a parent’s permission to loosen any of it ([Meta Family Center](https://familycenter.meta.com/instagram-safety/)). An under-16 TikTok account has no direct messaging at all, is private by default and carries a 60-minute default limit ([TikTok to Oklahoma Senate, October 2024](https://oksenate.gov/sites/default/files/2024-10/TikTok%20Letter%20to%20Oklahoma%20SJC%2010.17.24.pdf)). YouTube’s under-18 defaults turn on bedtime and take-a-break reminders and turn off autoplay ([YouTube, January 2026](https://blog.youtube/inside-youtube/a-decade-of-building-for-teens-and-families/)). An adult account has none of this. The inference that a borrowed adult account carries no teen protection is ours, not a statement by any vendor, but it follows from how every vendor describes its own settings. A ban does not remove the child. It removes the protection.

**Where the children go.** The ban moved children to services it does not cover. eSafety measured daily use of messaging services rising from 40.5 to 52.3 per cent, and daily online gaming from 26.4 to 29 per cent ([eSafety, August 2026](https://www.esafety.gov.au/sites/default/files/2026-08/Early-days-Early-Insights-SMMA-Evaluation-Understanding-experiences-of-social-media-age-restrictions-August2026.pdf)). AI companions sit outside the ban entirely; the four eSafety examined had no real check of a user’s age ([UTS, March 2026](https://www.uts.edu.au/news/2026/03/when-ai-companionship-turns-dark)). The children the Swedish inquiry heard predicted exactly this: an age limit would move them to other, probably less regulated, platforms ([SOU 2026:35, section 4.5](https://www.regeringen.se/contentassets/d1b21d5210774bfba9797e08883c9633/sou-2026_35_pdf-a_webb.pdf)).

**What the child stops saying.** Researchers warn that a child on a banned platform becomes less likely to tell a parent about something frightening, because they believe they should not be there at all ([Euronews, January 2026](https://euronews.com/next/2026/01/29/australias-under-16-social-media-ban-how-children-are-already-finding-ways-around-it)). That is expert reasoning, not yet a measured effect. Sweden’s inquiry made the same point when it chose no penalties for children or parents: children must dare to tell. A plan the child can read, with reasons, is built to keep that door open. A ban the child is quietly breaking closes it.

**The enforcement war.** The money goes on the chase. Australia’s eSafety received A$16.2 million for the minimum-age programme in 2025/26 within a four-year allocation of A$45.7 million ([eSafety FOI LOG 161, January 2026](https://www.esafety.gov.au/sites/default/files/2026-01/LOG_161_Document_Set.pdf)); Ofcom’s Online Safety Act spend reached £64.1 million in 2024/25. In June 2026 Australia moved to double the maximum fine to A$99 million because, in the Prime Minister’s words, there were still too many children on social media. Each new rule produces a new workaround, which produces a new check, which produces a new workaround. There is no exit from that loop. Every krona spent in it is a krona not spent giving parents a tool that works. The alternative is to give families something they would rather use.

**The cliff edge.** A ban teaches nothing. The UN Committee on the Rights of the Child asks states to respect children’s "evolving capacities", to help parents favour "mutual empathy and respect, over prohibition or control", to teach digital literacy from preschool, and to limit robust age verification to goods and services illegal for children ([UNCRC General Comment 25](https://www.ohchr.org/en/documents/general-comments-and-recommendations/general-comment-no-25-2021-childrens-rights-relation)). The UK’s own Children’s Code, its design rules for online services children use, protects children within the digital world, not from it. 5Rights warns that a social-media-only ban moves children to games, AI chatbots and learning apps ([5Rights, February 2026](https://5rightsfoundation.com/access-limitations-must-be-part-of-age-appropriate-design-and-effectively-restrict-companies-from-exploiting-children/)).

Two things this paper does not argue. It does not argue that screens are harmless; the research on harm is contested and both sides are summarised under Risks. Handrail does not need screens to be harmful to be worth doing. It needs childhood to be a time of learning. And it does not argue that parents are wrong to want a ban. They want their children safe and the fighting to stop. The question is whether a ban can deliver that alone.

## Where parents are today

A parent of two children with a tablet, a games console and a phone holds four or five separate apps: Google Family Link, Apple Screen Time, Microsoft Family Safety, and the Nintendo and PlayStation parent apps. None of them can see the others. Family Link counts time per device, so two hours means two hours on each. Microsoft removed the instant lock from Family Safety without notice. The PlayStation Family app works only with the console; no other app can connect to it. Apple lets approved apps set limits through its Screen Time API ([Apple](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/familycontrols)), but only on Apple devices, and the usage data cannot leave the device. No other major vendor offers a public way for other apps to connect to its parental controls. Volunteer coders reach two of them only through unofficial back routes that break the vendors’ terms and stop working when the vendor changes them.

Each app offers the same basic controls: a daily limit, a bedtime, an allow list, a lock. None has a notion of a child, only of a device or an account. None can grant minutes earned on one screen for use on another, and none can tell a parent what another vendor did. The household’s plan, where one exists at all, lives in a parent’s head.

Handrail does not replace these apps, and it is not a sixth one. Each vendor keeps its own app and its own design. What changes is that every vendor’s service follows the same single plan and reports back in the same way, so a limit set once holds across the console, the tablet and the phone, and a parent sees a single honest picture. To carry the mark, a device or service must follow the plan, report back what it did and keep the child’s record under the household’s control.

**The family chooses the controller: the app or box that holds the plan and sends it to every screen.** A Google family can make Family Link the main controller: once certified, it holds each child’s plan and talks to the Switch, the PlayStation and the Windows laptop, and shows what each of them did. Another family keeps one app per device, each built to report to the others or to a home hub. A third keeps a home hub that holds all the children’s plans and tells each device what to do. The family decides how much the pieces talk to each other, and may keep the children’s plans with different companies, or all in one place. The vendors’ services work the same way whichever setup the family picks. Each must accept a plan from any certified controller, may not insist that the parent uses its own app, and may not give another company’s controller fewer controls than its own. Handrail itself puts no app in front of parents. It publishes the standard, the tests a product must pass, and free, open example software that any maker can build on.

**Moving is a right.** The plans and the child’s record belong to the family, not to whoever runs the controller. A certified controller, a vendor’s or anyone else’s, keeps them only under the family’s own account and uses them only to run the plan. At any time, free and without asking why, it must hand the family everything in the standard format: every plan, every rule and its reason, the milestones the parent has recorded, and any part of the record not yet due for deletion. Any other certified controller must be able to take that file in and carry on where the last one stopped, with the same rules and the same history. For the parent the move is one step. The new controller takes over the plan on every platform, the old one can no longer change anything, and the child sees the hand-over in the record. Once the move is confirmed, the old controller deletes its copy unless the family asks it to keep one. The certification tests check this. A controller that cannot hand over a family’s file, or take one in from another controller, does not get the mark.

## Beside the ban: a floor and a path

The statutory minimum age stays, and platforms apply it under the law exactly as they would without Handrail. The child whose parents never engage, never install anything and never make a decision gets what the law gives them, with or without Handrail. Handrail adds nothing to that and takes nothing from it. How a platform finds that child is the law’s age-check question, not this standard’s.

One limit on Handrail. A platform applies a plan only to an account it knows is a child’s. The plan itself tells the platform this, carried on a family account the parent already holds. For accounts with no plan, Handrail changes nothing about how the law is enforced. Whether a plan, or an age range the parent gives on a family account, counts as enough for the accounts that carry one is for the regulator to say. The four asks near the end of this paper include one clause for that.

A parent who does engage can do more. Through a certified controller, the family account carries a plan, and the platform follows it. A plan can even let a child in below the minimum age, but only where the law allows a certified plan to do that. Australia’s law is an Act of Parliament with no exception for parents, so there the path needs the Act to be changed ([Federal Register of Legislation](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2024A00127/asmade/text)). Canada’s June 2026 bill already contains an opt-out for platforms that show protective policies ([TechCrunch, July 2026](https://techcrunch.com/2026/07/21/social-media-ban-children-countries-list/)). Handrail is that idea made specific enough to certify.

**Switched on by a parent.** A parent or guardian switches Handrail on by choice, in whichever certified controller they use: the vendor’s own app or an independent app, website or home hub. The family account they already hold with Apple, Google or another vendor is what shows they are the guardian, and the plan covers only the children on that account. The same parent switches it off in the same place; an adult with no plan is simply an adult. Where a law sets a minimum age, certified platforms apply it to child accounts by default, so Handrail complies with a limit. It does not need one to be useful. It was designed for a two year old with a tablet, long before any ban.

**What "good with screens" means.** The standard does not define it; parents do. But a shared vocabulary lets a parent, a guide and a study talk about the same thing. Handrail proposes six milestones. They are descriptions, not ages, and they are judgements a parent records as a name and a date, never scores a system assigns.

1. **Stopping.** Ends a session when told at first, later when the timer says, and in the end by choice. Observable: stopped at the limit without a lock on most school days this month.
2. **Choosing.** Picks apps and content for a reason they can state. Observable: can say what they made this week, not only what they watched.
3. **Meeting.** Plays and talks with known people online and knows what a stranger is. Observable: can say what they would do if a stranger messaged them, and who they would tell.
4. **Noticing.** Recognises a design built to hold them and can name it: autoplay, streaks, endless feed, loot boxes.
5. **Judging.** Weighs what they see. Observable: can tell an advert from a friend’s post.
6. **Owning.** Sets their own limits and keeps them most of the time. Observable: has chosen their own bedtime for a school week and kept it.

No part of Handrail ever records a child telling a parent about a worrying message. If telling is scored, children stop telling. The positive evidence comes from research on how parents guide children online, which the harm debate skips: strict limits lower online risk but also lower skills and chances, while talking, guiding and allowing ("enabling mediation") keeps skills and protects as children grow ([Livingstone and Helsper, 2008](https://doi.org/10.1080/08838150802437396); [Livingstone et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1111/jcom.12277)). Handrail is enabling mediation written down.

**One child’s path.** Call her Maja. At four she has a tablet with three apps her parents chose, and the screen shows in pictures how much is left. At seven she asks for a game; the guide tells her parents that other parents have labelled it creative and fine to play offline, and that it sells extras inside the game. They allow it, purchases need approval, and its time counts half against her limit because it is building, not scrolling. At ten she wants voice chat with school friends; the guide shows that playing only with friends is a different thing from chat with strangers, and suggests words for the talk about strangers, because many parents find it hard to start. At thirteen she wants a social account; the guide proposes a starter plan, private by default, no endless feed, no notifications after eight, an hour a day, with every rule visible to her, and the platform reports back what it actually did. At fourteen the guide notices three late nights in a row and suggests to Maja, not to her parents, that she move her own cut-off earlier. Her parents recorded the Noticing milestone that spring, and from then on they see only her total time against her limit; which apps she used is for her alone. Sleep is the one harm from screens that study after study has found ([Carter et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapediatrics.2016.2341)), so bedtime is the first rule the guide protects and the last it hands over. At sixteen there is nothing to switch off. The last rules end on a date she and her parents agreed, not one a statute chose, and she chooses whether to export or delete her milestone summary. The default is deletion.

&#91;embedded content: one child's path · who sets the rules, what parents can see\]

Rules move from parent to child as competence is shown, and what parents can see narrows as the child grows. The law’s own limit still covers any account with no plan, so nothing here depends on every parent engaging.

**The parent and the guide.** Many parents cannot do this alone. The guide is an AI assistant that works for the family. It reads the child’s record and what other parents have shared, proposes, explains, and never decides. It never reads content, messages, location or browsing, and a product must keep to that rule to be certified. It runs on the household’s own device or hub by default. If any part runs on a company’s computers elsewhere, only a small set of facts, listed in advance, leaves the home. It is not kept and not used to train AI. One caution: paediatric guidance warns that using screens as a reward raises their value to the child ([American Academy of Pediatrics](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/media/Pages/default.aspx)). Extra time given as a reward stays in the standard, because parents will give it anyway, but the guide points out when screen time is being paid for chores, and the ready-made plans do not include it.

**The AI guide is optional.** A household can run Handrail with no guide at all. The plan, the check on what each device actually did, the child’s view and the floor all work without it. Nothing is sent to any AI service unless a parent switches a guide on, and the parent decides what it sees. The guide has three positions.

**Off.** The default. No guide is installed or enrolled. Nothing leaves the controller for any AI purpose. The plan runs anyway.

**Sentry.** The guide may observe and suggest. It never acts. It may receive only the fields the parent ticks from this list, and nothing else: minutes used against budget, per child, per day; the names of the apps in the plan and which rule applies to each; milestone names and the dates the parent set them; the tags other parents have given apps the household is considering; and the plan’s own rules and reasons. It may suggest to the parent, or to the child where the parent allows it, for example an earlier bedtime after three late nights. A suggestion is a message. It changes nothing until a person accepts it.

**Control.** Everything in sentry, plus the guide may act, but only inside limits the parent writes down in advance: for example, grant up to 15 minutes once a day; move today’s cut-off earlier, never later; pause a single app for the rest of the day. The guide may never loosen a bedtime, unlock a locked device, add an app to the plan, change a budget upward or change a milestone. Every action is a temporary change (an override) that ends at a set time. It is logged with the guide named as the one who acted, and shown to the child in the same words the parent sees. The child can contest it as they can contest any override.

**Never, in any mode.** Messages or their content. Browsing history. Location, beyond the guardian’s declared country already in the plan. Contacts or friend lists. The child’s name, date of birth or any identifier. Photos, audio or video. Device identifiers. The standard gives the guide no way to receive any of these. A guide that asks for one is not a Handrail guide.

**Where it runs, and how it is certified.** On the household’s own device or hub by default. If any part runs remotely, only the ticked fields leave the home, with no retention beyond the request, no training on household data and no sharing with any third party. The controller shows the child, in plain words, that a guide is on, which mode it is in and which fields it receives. A guide is certified and listed like a platform: it must state in writing exactly which items of information it asks for and ask for nothing else, refuse any item outside the standard’s list, and in control mode act only within the limits the parent set and report back on every action. The test suite sends a guide fields it must refuse and fails it if it accepts them, and sends a control-mode guide an action outside its envelope and fails it if the action is not rejected. A guide that fails in real use receives notice, 90 days to put it right and an appeal before it is removed from the list, on the same terms as a platform.

## How the standard works

Handrail is a vocabulary and a set of rules for exchanging a child’s plan between a household and a platform, as IARC, the shared age-rating scheme, is for ratings and Matter, the shared smart-home standard, is for the smart home. It is not a product. It is the thing products must agree on.

&#91;embedded content: actors and the home boundary · what crosses, what never does\]

Everything about a child lives inside the home. Only the effects of a plan cross to a platform, under an identifier unique to that platform. Only app identifiers and anonymous tag votes ever reach the shared community database, and only if a parent turns sharing on.

**Who is involved.**

- **The child.** The person the data is about. Sees every rule and every reason.
- **The guardians.** Parents or carers. Hold the only right to set and change the plan.
- **The controller.** The certified app, website or hub a parent uses to write the plan and read the results: a vendor’s own app once certified, or an independent one. It holds the plan and the child’s full record under the family’s own account. The family chooses it, can move to another at any time and take everything with it, and no platform receives that record.
- **The platform.** Any operating system, console, store or service a child uses. Receives the parts of the plan that apply to it, applies them, and reports back what it did.
- **The guide.** An AI assistant working for the household. Optional. Certified like a platform. Reads only from the controller, and only what the parent allows.
- **The community database.** A shared, open database of app tags and ready-made profiles, holding nothing about any child.
- **The steward.** The non-profit that publishes the standard, runs the certification mark, gives each certified controller its proof of certification, and hosts the community database.
- **The regulator.** Publishes the official default plan for its country, recognises the mark, may require it, and audits the steward.

**What is exchanged.** Three kinds of message move between the home and a platform. Each has a part the platform receives and a part that never leaves the home. A **policy** is the plan’s standing rules for one child. The platform receives a daily budget rounded to 15 minutes, allowed windows in 30-minute slots, a bedtime, the highest content rating allowed, an age band in the same groups Google Play’s Age Signals service already uses (under 13, 13 to 15, 16 to 17, 18 and over), the guardian’s declared country so the right floor applies, and rules for named apps. Each rule says one of six things: allow always, allow, count half, count double, require approval, block. A platform receives only the parts of the plan it has said it can carry out. Milestones, progress, the guide’s reasoning, rules keyed to community tags and the exact budget stay home. An **override** is one parental decision with a lifetime: the platform receives "lock, this account, until 18:00" and never the reason or who decided. Every override expires, by default at the end of the day, so a punishment cannot quietly become permanent. A **result** is the platform’s honest answer, read back from its own state: applied, confirmed (shown to the parent as done), partial (partly), failed (could not) or unknown (do not know, and never shown as unlocked). Usage flows back as plain facts, and a child’s request as an app identifier plus a type, with no free text. Each platform gets its own code for the child, as OpenID Connect (the open standard behind many “sign in with” buttons) already does ([OpenID](https://openid.net/specs/openid-connect-core-1_0.html#PairwiseAlg)): no two platforms receive the same code, and no code for the household is sent at all.

**What a plan can say, and what it may not block.** A plan covers what as well as how long. A parent may allow or block apps and sites by identifier; block topics by name from the community vocabulary (gambling, loot boxes, dieting, violence, chat with strangers, and whatever else the family decides); block words and phrases in the child’s own languages; and copy a community profile such as "Swedish health guidance" or "educational first", then change any line. Inside the family’s controller, topic and word rules are turned into lists of apps and sites, so a platform receives only allow and block instructions, never the family’s reasons or words. One limit on parents is deliberate and fixed by the standard. A short list stays reachable on every certified device whatever the plan says: emergency numbers (112 in Sweden), the children’s helpline (Bris, 116 111), healthcare advice (1177), the non-emergency police number (114 14), the suicide line (Mind, 90101), information on how to report abuse, and information about children’s rights. A parent may filter how these appear but cannot remove them. A certified controller refuses a rule that would block them, and the test suite checks it. Which further topics belong on the protected list, for example sexual and reproductive health information for older children, is a decision for the steward with children’s organisations at the table.

**What stays in the home.** Two kinds of record. The daily record holds every minute, grant, lock and reason, written so a parent can show it to a child in full, and is deleted on a schedule the household chooses, default 31 days. The persistent summary holds milestone names and the dates a parent set them, and nothing else. What parents can see shrinks as their control does. Until a milestone the household sets, by default Noticing, parents and child both see detail for each app. After it, parents see only totals against budget and whether the rules are being applied, and only the child sees detail for each app. When a child leaves the plan, they choose export or deletion of the summary, the default is deletion, and everything else is deleted. This is how the standard meets the Children’s Code, whose standard 11 requires that children know when they are monitored, and General Comment 25, which discourages covertly documenting a child’s activity. Handrail documents overtly and shows the child.

**How community knowledge flows.** Parents know things ratings boards do not. Handrail carries a curated vocabulary of about thirty tags in five groups: value (educational, creative, reading), social (friends-only, chat-with-strangers, voice-chat), commerce (in-app purchases, loot boxes), content, and attention (endless feed, autoplay, no stopping point, offline-ok). Households vote, one vote per household per tag per app, using blind-signed tokens, a method that means no vote can be linked to a household or to another vote. A tag shows when more households vote for it than against; warning tags need evidence and three contributors; visible tags are capped at ten per app; a developer may dispute a tag on its own product. Parents publish named profiles ("strict under 10", "educational first", "Swedish health guidance") and others subscribe. The family’s own plan always wins over any shared profile or tag. Tags are resolved to app identifiers inside the controller and never reach a platform. The data is open, and a full copy is published every night, so nothing depends on the service staying up, and it never sees a child. Because it starts empty, the steward curates the 200 to 500 most-used children’s apps before launch and marks those tags "curated" until household votes replace them.

## What a platform must do to comply

A platform at Level 2 (the budget level in the table below) has a limited, clear job: accept a plan, apply it, check what it actually did, and report honestly.

A certified platform must:

- Accept a plan and an override for a child’s account from a controller whose credential the steward issued, checking the controller’s digital signature, never checking the parent. The guardian is checked once, by the family account on the device’s operating system (such as Apple’s or Google’s), which already holds the age declaration. The controller’s proof of certification depends on that check and can be withdrawn.
- Apply the law’s own minimum age to child accounts as the law requires, with or without Handrail. Where the regulator has published an official default plan in its public list (the registry), use it as the written form of that duty.
- For each child, take the plan from only one controller at a time. The parent chooses which, from the family account they already have. If the parent moves to a different controller, the old one can still read the child’s record but can no longer change the rules, and the move is written in the record where the child can see it.
- Apply the plan, read back its own state, and report the result honestly. Unknown is reported as unknown, never as unlocked. Failing without saying so breaks the standard.
- Report usage to the household, per app where it can, and say when it cannot. Carry a child’s structured request back to the household.
- Show the child one fixed notice: "A household plan applies. Set by your guardian. Open your household plan for the reason." Reasons, who decided, and any disagreement stay in the household’s own controller, never on the platform’s screen.
- Use what it receives only to apply the plan, check and record what it did, prevent abuse and run child-safety features. Never for advertising, measurement, building profiles across services, sale or sharing. Records of those checks carry a code, not a name, hold no content, and are kept for at most twelve months.
- Never ask for a date of birth, an identity document or a location through the Handrail channel. The only age source is the guardian’s existing OS family-account declaration, consumed as a band.

A certified controller must hold the plans and the child’s record only under the family’s own account and use them only to run the plan; export all of it in the standard format whenever a guardian asks, at no charge; import a household exported by any other certified controller; and, on a move, stop changing the plan once the new controller has confirmed, then delete its copy unless the family asks it to keep one. It must also watch home internet traffic only for the apps the plan names, keep no record of sites visited beyond the count of minutes, enrol a device only by explicit action visible to the child, exclude school-managed and employer-managed devices by default, and hold no login for another company’s service; it reaches other platforms only through the standard. A controller that logs in as the parent and uses a company’s private, unpublished connections is not certified. A guide must run in the home by default and, if any part is remote, receive only budget state, tag lookups and milestone names and dates, with no retention and no training.

&#91;embedded content: the honest result loop · one override from request to expiry\]

This loop of apply, check and report is the heart of trust. A platform that cannot check what it did says so, the parent and the child see the same three lines on their screens, and a child is never shown as free when a lock is in force.

There are five levels of compliance, so a simple home router and a large social network can both take part.

| Level | Name | What it must do | Who is there in 2027 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | Observer | Report minutes used | Household routers, smart plugs, the reference controller |
| 1 | Cut-off | Stop now, release later, confirm it | Household routers, smart plugs, the reference controller |
| 2 | Budget | Accept a daily allowance and windows, confirm by read-back | Nobody yet |
| 3 | Interactive | Level 2 plus grants, child requests and per-app rules | Nobody yet |
| 4 | Native | Accept full plans and overrides through one standard connection, and report back in the standard way | Nobody yet |

No major vendor offers other apps full access to its parental controls today; Apple’s Screen Time API comes closest, on Apple devices only. Where they match, Handrail uses the same names as Matter’s content controls, so a Matter television could join without changes once that feature, still marked provisional, reaches certified products, and its certification sits alongside ISO/IEC 27566-1, the new age-assurance framework standard ([ISO](https://www.iso.org/standard/88143.html)).

**Certification** follows OpenID Connect: a platform runs a published self-service test suite and submits the result, and the steward lists it publicly with level, date and suite version ([OpenID Foundation](https://openid.net/certification-accomplishments-and-next-steps/)). A controller runs the same suite, and part of it is a move: export a test household, import it into a different certified controller, and check that the plans, rules, reasons and history are the same afterwards. Spot checks of products in use are done with made-up test households only. A platform that, more often than a published limit, reports a rule as done without checking, or shows unknown as unlocked, receives written notice, 90 days to put it right and an independent appeal before it is removed from the list. Recognition across jurisdictions follows IARC: one certified result, accepted by every participating regulator ([IARC](https://www.globalratings.com/milestones)).

## Why each party gains

Everyone keeps what they have and gains something they lack.

- **The minister** keeps the law’s age limit, its list of covered services and its fine, and adds one more sentence: "A parent-managed account on a certified platform may carry a household plan that loosens step by step as the child grows." The floor covers the child nobody is helping; the path covers the child whose parents want to help.
- **The regulator** gets something auditable: a public registry, conformance levels, annual re-test, a published enforcement process, and a steward it can inspect. Ofcom’s first age-assurance report says protecting children needs a system-wide effort, and asks what role operating systems and app stores should play; Handrail is one answer, written down.
- **The Treasury** gets a case for mutual recognition, where countries accept each other’s approval. Fifteen jurisdictions now name different ages, service lists and penalties, from A$49.5 million to 6 per cent of global turnover, and every platform answers every regulator alone. One mark recognised everywhere, as IARC is for ratings, is a competitiveness argument a finance ministry will own.
- **Parents** get one place to set one plan for all their child’s screens, a guide that knows the games, and the step-by-step loosening they already want.
- **Children** get visible steps, a record they can read, a way to contest a rule, privacy from their parents that grows with them, and an end date.
- **Platforms** get one shared standard to meet and, if the EU agrees, a safe harbour: protection for a platform that applies the plan honestly. "We support Handrail Level 2" is one sentence for fifteen regulators. An account that arrives with a plan need not be age-verified by the platform, wherever the regulator accepts the age range held by the device’s operating system, together with the plan. The OS vendors are the natural first implementers: Apple’s Declared Age Range API ([Apple](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/declaredagerange)) and Google’s Play Age Signals API ([Android Developers Blog, July 2026](https://android-developers.googleblog.com/2026/07/google-play-age-signals-api-safer-experiences.html)) already carry a parent-set age band to apps. Apple argued in 2025 that store-wide age verification would force all users to hand over sensitive identifying information ([Apple](https://developer.apple.com/support/downloads/Helping-Protect-Kids-Online-2025.pdf)); Google’s policy lead made the same privacy argument against Utah’s app-store law ([Pluribus News, March 2025](https://pluribusnews.com/news-and-events/google-pitches-age-verification-alternative/)). The ask is small: the same route that already passes the age range to apps also passes the plan, and sends back what the app did.

Standards that companies adopted even though they did not want to at first shared four features, and the ones that died lacked all four.

The arithmetic is slow. Two members at the middle tier plus two certifications is under 50,000 USD a year. Dues cover 600,000 EUR only at roughly 25 members, which on this roadmap is 2031 at the earliest. A government and sponsor line stays until that member count exists. If two independent products have not passed the tests by 2028, the specification stays open in the Community Group and households keep what they have.

Principles. A permanent child-rights seat and a permanent privacy seat on the board. Platform seats capped at half. The founding company (the startup described below) holds at most one non-voting seat, its own products are certified by the same suite and listed with the same disclosure as everyone else, and a full copy of the community service’s code and data is kept outside the company from day one. The steward never holds per-child data, never charges parents or children, and never sells data.

Honest weakness: Signal’s foundation ran an 8.6 million USD deficit in 2024 on 29 million USD of revenue ([ProPublica](https://projects.propublica.org/nonprofits/organizations/824506840)). Donation funding is fragile. Until certification fees come in, the only payer is a government, or nobody. That is why the Roadmap turns this gap into a costed request.

## The startup

The steward runs the standard itself. A company builds and sells the first certified controller and guide, and does not wait for any platform to adopt anything.

The company’s product is a paid household subscription from 2027: a certified controller and guide working over the devices and network controls a household already owns, holding no copy of any child’s record, which lives in the home. Its lasting advantage over rivals is not the open specification or the open data, which anyone may take. It is cross-vendor neutrality, which no OS vendor can offer, the guide’s quality, and the installed base of households. A regulator naming Handrail in 2029 would add to that advantage, but the company does not need it to earn money. The main cost that grows with each household is the computing power for any part of the AI guide that runs outside the home, and it must appear in the business plan.

Today’s parental controls are apps tied to one company, or subscriptions tied to one device. None can be run from anyone else’s app, and all rely on unofficial workarounds that break. A product that is certified, neutral, and whose limits on data are set by a published standard rather than its own privacy policy is a different thing to sell to a parent, and a different thing to put in front of a regulator.

The funding route from Sweden runs through safety-tech and digital-childhood grants (Vinnova, Innovate UK, EU Horizon, the UK Safety Tech Challenge Fund, Australia’s eSafety grants, foundations such as Omidyar and Nesta) for the open parts, and conventional seed capital for the company. The detail, including the competitive map, market sizing and company structure, is kept out of this policy paper on purpose and lives in a separate funding note.

## Roadmap and the four asks

Sweden’s inquiry delivers its final report, on age verification, on 14 November 2026, and the proposed law would start on 1 January 2028. No bill can come before the final report, and no certified platform can exist before 2028. Nothing here asks Sweden to wait or to drop the limit. Four asks fit between the report and the bill.

&#91;embedded content: roadmap 2027 to 2029 · three asks, one gate, one lever\]

1. **One clause in the bill that follows SOU 2026:35.** It recognises certified household plans and lets the government name an authority to say which certifications count. It puts no new duty on platforms, which the inquiry found Sweden cannot do under the DSA. It commits the state to nothing until the Handrail mark exists, and it does not reopen the age decision.
2. **A published default plan.** Ask the Public Health Agency (Folkhälsomyndigheten) to publish its hours by age as the default Swedish household plan, so every certified controller, whoever built it, starts from the same national guidance, and ask Mediemyndigheten to name the mark in guidance once a platform is certified.
3. **A Swedish pilot.** 400 households, evaluated by a Swedish university under its own ethics approval, results published whatever they show. About 3.5 million EUR for the whole two-year programme, costed below.
4. **One ask in Brussels before 3 December 2026.** The KIDS Act proposal already says the largest platforms’ tools for guardians must work with other companies’ tools (Article 20(5)) ([COM(2026) 681](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:52026PC0681)). Ask that a certified open standard counts as meeting that rule, that platforms applying it honestly are protected, and that the parent-controlled account for 13 and 14 year olds can be run by any certified controller. The Ministry of Culture leads on the file and consults stakeholders in November 2026.

The university holds the pilot data and is legally responsible for it under data protection law, with separate consent for each child and the child’s own agreement, asked in words suited to their age, no link to the community service, and deletion at study end. What a government gets for it is the evidence nobody has: of the 40 restriction experiments reviewed by Lind, Schueller and Odgers, none reported a mean participant age under 18 ([Frontiers, May 2026](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fdpys.2026.1805989/full)). The pilot produces consented, minimal, longitudinal data on how children acquire screen competence, published whether or not it flatters the idea. Outcome measures are sleep, self-reported wellbeing, media literacy, parent-child conflict and the six milestones, not account counts or minutes.

**2027** publishes Handrail 1.0 as a W3C Community Group draft with schemas, conformance rules and the tag vocabulary, releases the reference controller as open source, names the pilot sponsor, pre-registers the pilot design, and briefs Ofcom, eSafety, the European Commission’s team for minors under the Digital Services Act (DSA) and the Swedish commission with one question each: if a platform certified at Level 2, would you say so in guidance? **2028** brings the conformance suite and registry live, one OS vendor or console maker at Level 2, the community service with curated tags, the steward incorporated once two implementations conform, and first pilot results. **2029** is when one regulator, anywhere, names Handrail conformance in guidance, a code of practice or a procurement document; a second platform certifies; membership opens; and the first outcome report is published, comparing results with the ban alone. If no regulator names Handrail by 2029, the standard remains useful to the households that run it, and the specification stays open for whoever picks it up next.

## Risks and objections, with answers

The strongest objection is the last one in this list, and the pilot exists to test it.

- **"Parents want a ban."** Around nine in ten of the UK parents whose consultation answers were broken out did. Handrail does not take it from them. The floor stays; the path is for parents who want more.
- **"Platforms never adopt open standards voluntarily."** Correct. No vendor exposed an age signal until Texas and Utah made them. Handrail does not ask for goodwill. It rides the laws that already force parent-mediated channels and asks the regulator to name the mark. Until then the reference controller works over the household’s own devices and network controls without anyone’s permission.
- **"Apple and Google could ship this themselves."** They are the foundation, not the competitor, and they could. Screen Time and Family Link are controllers already, and once certified either one can set the plan for a PlayStation or a Windows PC. What neither can be is the referee. No household is single-vendor, so a regulator still needs a mark not owned by one of the companies it regulates, and every other vendor needs one test to pass rather than one deal per rival.
- **"A parent signal can be faked."** So can a ban, and Australia shows how often. Handrail’s floor is no weaker than the ban's; its path serves the willing majority. The pilot measures how many route around a plan they helped write.
- **"The AI guide is surveillance."** It would be if it read content or ran in a vendor’s cloud on the whole record. The standard gives it no field for content, messages or location, requires it to run in the home by default, and certifies it. The ledger is visible to the child by design, which is what the Children’s Code requires. The weakness is real: a conformance mark constrains the standard, not what a vendor does beside it.
- **"The milestones are a rough stand-in dressed up as a measurement."** Agreed, if they were a measurement. Each is a parental judgement the record informs, held only in the home, and sent to no platform. The pilot tests whether ledger patterns predict anything a researcher would accept.
- **"The evidence is too early to judge Australia."** Agreed. This paper says the early evidence is weak, not that the policy has failed. The review point exists to judge both instruments on the same evidence.
- **"Competence milestones are a middle-class fantasy."** The strongest objection. Default profiles, a guide that does the thinking, and community profiles from households like one’s own are the answers. They may not be enough. The pilot must include families across income, education and shared-device households, and the data must be published either way.

**The harm evidence, both sides.** Kept short because it is politically unusable and scientifically contested. Orben and Przybylski found technology use explains at most 0.4 per cent of variance in adolescent wellbeing ([Nature Human Behaviour, 2019](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-018-0506-1)); Odgers’ review found a mix of no, small and mixed associations ([Nature, 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-024-00902-2)); Lind, Schueller and Odgers reviewed 40 restriction studies and found none with a mean participant age under 18, and disclose industry advisory ties ([Frontiers, 2026](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fdpys.2026.1805989/full)). On the other side, Braghieri, Levy and Makarin found a causal effect of Facebook’s rollout on student mental health ([AER, 2022](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.20211218)), and Allcott and colleagues found modest wellbeing gains from a month off Facebook ([AER, 2020](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.20190658)). The one finding replicated across camps is sleep displacement from bedtime device use, which is why bedtime is the rule Handrail protects first.

**Weaknesses in our own evidence.** Several primary pages blocked automated fetching, so figures were cross-checked against two secondary reports each and must be re-read from source before publication. The BMJ sample is 408 children in one state. VPN figures are vendor-reported. The parental mediation and sleep citations were added in synthesis and should be checked against the papers. The cost table is a planning estimate.
