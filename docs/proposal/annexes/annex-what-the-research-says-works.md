# Annex: What the research says works for children's screen time

*Supplementary paper to the Handrail proposal. Checked against sources on 22 October 2026.*

## What this adds

The main Handrail paper rests on three research claims. Strict limits lower risk but also lower skills. Talking and guiding ("enabling mediation") works better as children grow. Sleep is the clearest harm. This annex goes back to the studies behind those claims and adds the Swedish and Nordic evidence the main paper does not cover. It sets out what parental control tools have been shown to do, what monitoring does to trust, and what the evidence on bedtime rules actually shows. Sources are graded as strong or weak. Two claims in the main paper need softer wording, and the annex says which. It ends with design rules for Handrail and a list of what the 400-household pilot should measure.

A note on terms. **Parental mediation** is the research name for what parents do to shape a child's media use. **Restrictive mediation** means rules and limits: how long, what, when. **Active mediation** means talking about media, explaining and helping. **Enabling mediation** is a broader mix of active mediation, safety efforts and letting the child explore. **Effect size** means how big a difference is, not just whether it exists. A correlation (r) of 0.1 is small and 0.3 is medium. An **odds ratio** of 2 means the odds of the outcome are about twice as high in one group.

## 1. Parental mediation: restriction versus talking

**The core European findings.** The research the main paper cites holds up, but it is more mixed than the main paper says.

- Livingstone and Helsper surveyed 1,511 UK children aged 12 to 17 and 906 parents. Parents preferred active shared use and rules about online interaction over technical restrictions such as filters or monitoring software. Only limits on the child's online contact with peers went with lower risk. Shared use, which was widely practised, showed no such link ([Livingstone and Helsper, Journal of Broadcasting and Electronic Media, 2008, LSE abstract](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/25723/)).
- Livingstone and colleagues surveyed 6,400 parents of 6 to 14 year olds in eight European countries. They found two strategies. Enabling mediation went with **more online opportunities but also more risks**. Restrictive mediation went with fewer risks but fewer opportunities. Restriction was more common where the parent or child had weaker digital skills ([Livingstone et al., Journal of Communication, 2017, LSE record](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/68612/)).

**A correction for the main paper.** The main paper says enabling mediation "keeps skills and protects as children grow". The 2017 study does not show that it protects. It shows that it goes with more opportunity and more risk. The abstract adds that, because enabling mediation is used where the parent or child is fairly skilled, it "may not support harm" (the authors' phrase, which is not fully clear). Risk is not the same as harm: a child who meets some risk and learns to handle it may be better off. But the paper should say "builds skills, at the cost of some extra exposure to risk", not "protects".

**How big are the effects?** Small. A meta-analysis (a study that pools the results of many studies) of 57 studies by Collier and colleagues found that restrictive mediation had a small link to child outcomes (r = -0.06) and co-viewing (watching together) a small link the other way (r = 0.09). Active mediation overall showed no significant link. It did go with slightly less aggression (r = -0.08), sexual behaviour (r = -0.06) and substance use (r = -0.11) ([Collier et al., Developmental Psychology, 2016, BYU record](https://scholarsarchive.byu.edu/facpub/4021)). These are associations, not measured causes, and they are small enough that no parenting style should be sold as a cure.

**What EU Kids Online advises.** The 2020 EU Kids Online report covered 25,101 children aged 9 to 16 in 19 countries, with fieldwork from autumn 2017 to summer 2019. In most countries, most children said their parents talked to them, encouraged them or helped them online. But in several countries about half of even the youngest group (9 to 11) said their parents hardly ever or never talked to them about what they do online. The authors found restrictions linked to lower risk but also lower digital skills. They advised parents not to use restrictions alone, to explain why each rule exists, and to loosen rules as the child grows ([Smahel et al., EU Kids Online 2020, LSE, 2020, p. 120](https://eprints.lse.ac.uk/103294/1/EU_Kids_Online_2020_March2020.pdf)). Sweden does not appear in that report. A new EU Kids Online survey in 19 countries has been reported in 2026. One Irish report gives 28,465 children aged 10 to 16 ([Webwise, Ireland, undated, probably September 2026](https://www.webwise.ie/news/new-eu-kids-online-2026-survey/)). The German partner's project page describes a 2026 report on age bans from the same network with 29,169 children ([Leibniz-HBI project page](https://leibniz-hbi.de/en/projects/eu-kids-online)). The two figures do not match, and this annex could not explain why. It could not open the full 2026 report, so its parenting findings are not used here, and it could not confirm whether Sweden took part.

**Strength of evidence.** Moderate for the pattern (restriction lowers risk and opportunity; talking goes with skills). Weak for cause, because almost all of it comes from surveys taken at one point in time.

## 2. Parental control tools: what they actually do

**The best overview.** Stoilova, Bulger and Livingstone reviewed the research on parental control tools. From 1,656 search results they coded 40 studies. Outcomes were mixed: some benefit, some harm, some limits on other good outcomes, and some with no effect. They found "little support for advocating parental controls as a stand-alone strategy". Parents valued the tools when they were part of a wider approach built on the parent and child relationship ([Stoilova, Bulger and Livingstone, Journal of Children and Media, 2023, LSE record](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/120219/)). This is a rapid review, a quicker and less complete method than a full systematic review.

**Controls and teenagers.** A US online survey of 215 parent and teen pairs (teens aged 13 to 17) found that parents low in autonomy granting (giving teens room to decide for themselves), such as strict authoritarian parents, were the most likely to use control apps. Greater parental control went with **more** online risk, not less ([Ghosh et al., CHI 2018 Extended Abstracts, Penn State record](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/a-matter-of-control-or-safety-examining-parental-use-of-technical/)). The study cannot say which came first. Teens who had been victimised online and had problems with peers were more likely to be monitored, so parents may install apps after trouble starts.

**What the apps are built to do.** A study of 74 Android safety apps found that about 89 per cent of features were for parental control (blocking and monitoring) and about 11 per cent supported teenagers in managing their own behaviour ([Penn State news, 27 February 2017](https://www.psu.edu/news/research/story/online-security-apps-focus-parental-control-not-teen-self-regulation)). This is a university news report of the paper, not the paper itself.

**Swedish evidence.** The Public Health Agency's 2024 knowledge review, made in collaboration with Mediemyndigheten (the Swedish Agency for the Media), found that for gaming problems, "parental controls or restrictions on gaming are neither a risk factor nor a protective factor" (our translation). For children over 9, good relationships with parents and parents' insight into the child's gaming were protective. For children under five, a restrictive parental stance did protect against later problem use ([Folkhälsomyndigheten, Digitala medier och barns och ungas hälsa, 2024, article 24190](https://mediemyndigheten.se/globalassets/rapporter-och-analyser/2024/digitala-medier-och-barns-och-ungas-halsa.pdf)). The review rated its own findings only as "some" or "good" reliability. It did not use GRADE, the standard system for grading the strength of evidence, except in two Norwegian reviews it drew on.

**What Swedish parents do.** Mediemyndigheten's Föräldrar & medier 2025 sent surveys to 14,000 parents of children aged 0 to 18. Statistics Sweden (SCB) collected the data between 15 October 2024 and 6 February 2025. Talking was "by far" the most common way parents protected children aged 9 to 18. Use of built-in parental controls has risen steadily since 2018. Among parents of 5 to 8 year olds, the share who talk with the child about the internet fell from 70 per cent in 2022 to 58 per cent, and rules on how long the child may be online have fallen in all age groups under 9 ([Mediemyndigheten, Föräldrar & medier 2025, pp. 90 to 94](https://www.mediemyndigheten.se/globalassets/rapporter-och-analyser/ungar--medier/mediemyndigheten-rapport-foraldrar-och-medier.pdf)).

**Strength of evidence.** Weak to moderate. This annex found no randomised trial (a study where families are assigned to groups by chance) of a parental control tool with children. The consistent message is that tools on their own do little, and that they work best inside a relationship.

## 3. Monitoring, privacy and trust

Swedish research is an important source on this question.

**How parents really know things.** Kerr and Stattin looked at three ways parents learn about their teenager's life: setting rules, asking questions, and the teenager choosing to tell them. They found that what the teenager chooses to tell is the best predictor of what parents know. They also found that control went with the teen feeling overly controlled, which went with poorer, not better, adjustment. Later work by the same group found that parents' own efforts had little or no effect on what they knew. This summary is taken from a later Swedish paper that reviews their work, as the originals were not open to this annex. That paper also notes that findings on parents asking questions are mixed ([Kapetanovic et al., Journal of Family Studies, 2017, pp. 2 to 3](https://center.hj.se/download/18.3d2cde02178e4440c93466ca/1620390627577/Kapetanovich%20(2017)%20Structural%20relations.pdf)).

**A newer Swedish test.** Kapetanovic and colleagues studied 1,520 Swedish adolescents with an average age of 13. A child telling parents things went with lower levels of bullying, substance use and delinquency (minor crime such as theft or vandalism). Parents asking questions went with more risk behaviour, especially for boys, through the child feeling overly controlled. The authors conclude that open communication and enough independence matter most ([Kapetanovic et al., 2017, abstract](https://center.hj.se/download/18.3d2cde02178e4440c93466ca/1620390627577/Kapetanovich%20(2017)%20Structural%20relations.pdf)). This is a single-time survey, so it shows links, not causes, as the authors themselves say.

**What this means for screens.** Neither study is about apps. But the logic carries over. A tool that shows parents everything, without the child choosing to share, may reduce the very thing that keeps children safe: telling. The main paper's rule that Handrail never records a child telling a parent about a worrying message fits this evidence well.

**Strength of evidence.** Moderate for parenting in general: the finding that telling matters most recurs across studies, though findings on parents asking questions are mixed. Weak for digital monitoring tools in particular, because few studies test them directly.

## 4. Time limits, bedtime rules and sleep

**The association is strong and consistent.** A meta-analysis of 20 studies covering 125,198 children aged 6 to 19 found that using a device at bedtime went with about twice the odds of too little sleep (odds ratio 2.17), higher odds of poorer sleep (1.46) and nearly three times the odds of daytime sleepiness (2.72). Simply having a device in the bedroom at night, without using it, also raised the odds of too little sleep (1.79) ([Carter et al., JAMA Pediatrics, 2016, Stony Brook record](https://researchconnect.stonybrook.edu/en/publications/association-between-portable-screen-based-media-device-access-or-/)). The abstract describes the 20 studies it assessed as cross-sectional, meaning they measure at one point in time and cannot show cause.

**The Swedish review agrees, cautiously.** The Public Health Agency's review found that a lot of screen time can lead to falling asleep later, sleeping less and waking more often. Having a phone or tablet in the bedroom can lead to poorer and shorter sleep. The link can run both ways: poor sleepers may also use screens more at night. Of 234 search results, 23 reviews met its criteria on sleep, 16 were excluded for high risk of bias (a high chance that the method distorts the result) and, as printed, 9 were included. These numbers do not add up, and the report does not explain why ([Folkhälsomyndigheten, 2024, pp. 65 to 66](https://mediemyndigheten.se/globalassets/rapporter-och-analyser/2024/digitala-medier-och-barns-och-ungas-halsa.pdf)).

**Swedish children's own reports.** In Ungar & medier 2025, 23 per cent of 9 to 12 year olds used their phone at night, 10 per cent every night or several times a week. That rose to 39 per cent of 13 to 16 year olds and 54 per cent of 17 to 18 year olds using it every night or several times a week. The share who think their media use harms their sleep rose from 16 per cent at 9 to 12 to 46 per cent at 17 to 18. Mediemyndigheten concludes that night-time phone use is "a significant factor" behind the harms young people themselves report ([Mediemyndigheten, Ungar & medier 2025, pp. 19 to 20](https://www.mediemyndigheten.se/globalassets/rapporter-och-analyser/ungar--medier/mediemyndigheten-rapport-ungar-och-medier.pdf)).

**But experiments show smaller or no effects.**

| Study | Design | Who | What changed | Sleep result |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| SCREENS, Denmark ([Pedersen et al., JAMA Pediatrics, 2022, SDU thesis record](https://portal.findresearcher.sdu.dk/en/publications/recreational-screen-media-use-and-its-effect-on-physical-activity/); [Newswise, 23 May 2022](https://www.newswise.com/articles/reducing-screen-time-increases-physical-activity-in-children)) | Randomised trial, 2 weeks | 89 families, 181 children | Leisure screen use cut, phones and tablets handed in | No effect on children's sleep, measured by brain-wave recording (EEG). Children's non-sedentary leisure time rose by 45 minutes a day |
| He et al. ([PLOS ONE, 2020](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0228756)) | Randomised pilot, about 4 weeks | 38 students at one university in Shanghai, China, who already slept poorly | No phone in the 30 minutes before bed | Fell asleep about 12 minutes sooner, slept about 18 minutes longer, by sleep diary |
| Bauducco et al. ([Flinders University news, 7 June 2023](https://news.flinders.edu.au/blog/2023/06/07/new-insights-into-teen-sleep/)) | Survey at two time points, 2019 to 2020 | 2,509 Australian pupils aged 12 to 14 | Parent-set bedtimes kept or brought back | At least 20 minutes more sleep on average; bedtime rules seemed to slow the usual drift to later bedtimes |

The Danish trial is the strongest design and found no sleep effect, but it was short and cut daytime screen use, not only bedtime use. The other two are small or observational. Bauducco's study, led by a visiting researcher from Sweden, is notable for Handrail: it suggests that a **bedtime rule**, rather than a total time limit, is what goes with more sleep, and that some teenagers were happy for parents to bring bedtime rules back in the early teens.

**Rules in Swedish homes.** In Ungar & medier 2025, families more often set a time by which media must stop than a limit on how long. About 8 in 10 children aged 9 to 12 had rules on how late they could watch films or clips, play games or use the phone on weekdays. Rules on social media time for 9 to 12 year olds rose by 16 percentage points since 2022. Mediemyndigheten suggests this may reflect the screen time debate and the Public Health Agency's advice, launched while its data were being collected ([Mediemyndigheten, Ungar & medier 2025, pp. 138 to 139](https://www.mediemyndigheten.se/globalassets/rapporter-och-analyser/ungar--medier/mediemyndigheten-rapport-ungar-och-medier.pdf)).

**Bans in one setting.** A study of 1,227 pupils aged 12 to 15 in 30 English secondary schools compared schools with restrictive and permissive phone policies. Pupils at restrictive schools used their phones about 40 minutes less during the school day. There was no difference in total use on weekdays or weekends, or in wellbeing ([Goodyear et al., Lancet Regional Health Europe, February 2025, Birmingham record](https://research.birmingham.ac.uk/en/publications/school-phone-policies-and-their-association-with-mental-wellbeing/)). This was a one-point-in-time comparison, not a trial. A rule in one place may move use to another. That supports Handrail's case for one plan across all devices and places, though no study has yet tested whether a joined-up plan does better.

**A correction for the main paper.** The main paper calls sleep "the one harm from screens that study after study has found". That is fair for the link between bedtime devices and sleep. It is too strong if read as proven cause. Suggested wording: "the most consistent finding, though experiments so far show small or no effects".

**Strength of evidence.** Strong for the link between night-time devices and poor sleep. Weak for how much changing the rule improves sleep.

## 5. What the Swedish data show about families

**The guidance has moved behaviour, probably.** Children's own estimates of their daily device time on a usual day in their free time fell, for 9 to 12 year olds, from 4 hours 21 minutes in 2022 to 3 hours 43 minutes in 2024, a fall of 38 minutes. It fell 21 minutes at 13 to 16 and only 6 minutes at 17 to 18 ([Mediemyndigheten, Ungar & medier 2025, p. 9](https://www.mediemyndigheten.se/globalassets/rapporter-och-analyser/ungar--medier/mediemyndigheten-rapport-ungar-och-medier.pdf)). The main paper uses the press figure of 40 minutes, so it should say 38. The Public Health Agency says it has had confirmation that its advice "has made a difference" ([Folkhälsomyndigheten, 24 September 2025](https://www.folkhalsomyndigheten.se/nyheter-och-press/nyhetsarkiv/2025/september/skarmrekommendationer-minskar-barns-tid-vid-dator-och-mobil/)). That is the agency judging its own advice, and the screen debate happened at the same time. Response rates were 25.8 per cent for 9 to 12 year olds and 23.3 per cent for 13 to 18 year olds, so the figures are weighted to make up for missing groups ([Ungar & medier 2025, method](https://www.mediemyndigheten.se/globalassets/rapporter-och-analyser/ungar--medier/mediemyndigheten-rapport-ungar-och-medier.pdf)).

**Parents and children see things differently.** This matters for any system that reports to parents.

- Parents say they "often" talk with their child, about the child's day, media and other topics, about 10 to 20 percentage points more than children say so ([Föräldrar & medier 2025, pp. 44 to 45](https://www.mediemyndigheten.se/globalassets/rapporter-och-analyser/ungar--medier/mediemyndigheten-rapport-foraldrar-och-medier.pdf)).
- Internetstiftelsen (the Swedish Internet Foundation) had the polling firm Novus interview 629 parents and 625 children aged 8 to 19 online in January 2025. In mellanstadiet (ages 10 to 12), 58 per cent of parents but only 41 per cent of children said they argue about screen time. Parents with higher education reported more rows (40 per cent) than parents with only school-level education (29 per cent). Household income made no difference ([Internetstiftelsen, Barnen och internet 2025, pp. 5 and 22 to 23](https://svenskarnaochinternet.se/app/uploads/2025/11/internetstiftelsen-barnen-och-internet-2025.pdf)).
- Parents who avoid rows advised others to bring in screens late, with clear rules from the start applied consistently, to **involve the child in making the rules**, and to be a good example. Some children, mainly at secondary school, advised others to use the phone in secret so parents would not know their real screen time ([Internetstiftelsen, 2025, pp. 7, 25 and 31](https://svenskarnaochinternet.se/app/uploads/2025/11/internetstiftelsen-barnen-och-internet-2025.pdf)).

**Timing.** Mediemyndigheten's series has appeared every two years, and the 2025 editions use data collected from mid-October 2024 to early February 2025, a few weeks to a few months after the Public Health Agency's guidance of September 2024 ([Mediemyndigheten, Ungar och medier series page](https://mediemyndigheten.se/rapporter-och-analyser/ungar-och-medier/)). The full effect of the guidance will only be seen properly in the next edition. Nothing newer was listed as of 22 October 2026.

## 6. Strong and weak evidence at a glance

| Claim | Evidence | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| Devices at bedtime and in the bedroom go with worse sleep | Meta-analysis of 20 studies; Swedish review; Swedish child reports | Strong (association) |
| Removing screens improves children's sleep | One short randomised trial found no effect; one small pilot with university students found about 12 to 18 minutes by sleep diary | Weak |
| Bedtime rules go with more sleep in early teens | One large two-wave study, seen only through a university news report | Moderate |
| Restriction lowers risk but also skills | Large European surveys; small pooled effects | Moderate (association) |
| Talking and enabling builds skills | Large European surveys | Moderate (association) |
| Enabling mediation protects from harm | Not shown; it goes with more risk | Not supported |
| Parental control tools alone reduce risk | Rapid review of 40 studies; Swedish review on gaming | Not supported |
| Close control of teens goes with worse outcomes | Swedish surveys; US survey of control apps | Moderate (association) |
| What parents know comes mainly from children telling | Research by Kerr, Stattin and colleagues, as summarised in a 2017 Swedish paper | Moderate |
| A single plan across all devices works better than separate apps | No study found | Untested |

## 7. What this means for Handrail's design

1. **Rules with reasons, made together.** The evidence never supports restriction alone. Every rule the child sees should carry a reason, and the plan should record that the child took part. This is what EU Kids Online advises and what Swedish parents who avoid rows describe.
2. **Bedtime first, as the main paper says, but by clock time and place.** The strongest signal is about devices at night and in the bedroom, and about set bedtimes. A "devices off and out of the bedroom by" setting should be the default rule, ahead of daily totals.
3. **Firm limits for the youngest, loosening with age.** In the Swedish review, a restrictive stance protects under-fives against later problem use. EU Kids Online advises loosening as children grow. A schedule that loosens by default fits both.
4. **Report less than you can.** Control that the child feels as intrusive goes with worse outcomes, and what parents know comes mainly from telling. Totals against a limit are enough by default. Showing which apps or messages should need a reason and should be visible to the child.
5. **Give the child tools, not just the parent.** About 89 per cent of app features studied were for parental control. Handrail should give the child self-management features of their own, which the main paper's guide already starts to do.
6. **Do not promise large effects.** The best-measured effects are small. The honest claim is that Handrail makes existing good practice easier to apply across devices, not that it will transform outcomes.

## 8. What the 400-household pilot should measure

**Design.** A comparison group is essential, or the pilot cannot tell Handrail's effect apart from the national trend. One option is to randomise households to start now or start six months later (a "waitlist" design), so every family gets Handrail in the end. Pre-register the design and outcomes (publish them before the study starts), as the main paper proposes. As a rough guide (this annex's own calculation), two groups of about 175 households each give an 80 per cent chance of detecting a small to medium difference (about 0.3 standard deviations, a standard way of expressing a difference relative to the normal spread of scores). Smaller effects, like those in the mediation research, would be missed. The pilot should say so in advance.

**Outcomes, with the child as a source, not just the parent.**

| Outcome | How | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Sleep length and bedtime | Wrist-worn sleep tracker for one week at the start and end, plus a sleep diary | Self-report and parent report differ; the Danish trial used objective measures |
| Night-time phone use | Child question worded as in Ungar & medier ("Använder du mobilen på natten?", "Do you use your phone at night?") | Allows comparison with national figures |
| Screen-time rows | Same question to parent and child, worded as in Internetstiftelsen's survey | Parents and children report differently |
| Child telling parents | A short child-reported disclosure scale from the Swedish parenting research | The main protective factor in the evidence |
| Feeling over-controlled | Child-reported scale | The route by which control can backfire |
| Digital skills and opportunities | Child-reported items used in EU Kids Online | Tests the trade-off restriction causes |
| Risk experiences | Child-reported items used in Ungar & medier | Tests whether enabling raises risk |
| Getting round the plan | Child-reported, anonymous, and checked against the record | Children report hiding use |
| Who benefits | Results split by parental education, income, single-device homes and children with mental health conditions | Rows and protection methods differ by group in the Swedish data |

**What not to count as success.** Minutes saved, accounts created or features switched on. These do not show children are better off.

## Open questions

- The full EU Kids Online 2026 comparative report could not be opened. Its findings on parents talking with children, whether Sweden took part, and why two pages give different sample sizes (28,465 and 29,169) need checking before the annex cites them.
- The original Kerr and Stattin papers were not opened. Their findings are taken from a 2017 Swedish paper that reviews them. The findings, and who was studied, should be checked against the originals.
- The SCREENS trial's later mental health analysis (2024) is reported in news coverage but the paper could not be opened, so no effect size is given here.
- The Bauducco study was seen only through a university news report. Its figures should be checked against the paper in the Journal of Sleep Research.
- No randomised trial of a parental control tool with children, or of a single plan across devices, was found. Is there one this annex missed?
- How should a wrist-worn sleep tracker in the pilot be squared with Handrail's own rule against collecting more about the child than needed? The university's ethics approval would need to address this.
- Will Mediemyndigheten's next Ungar & medier survey (if fieldwork runs in autumn 2026) ask questions that let the pilot compare its households with the national picture?
