# Annex: How other countries handle parental controls and children's screens

*Supplementary paper to the Handrail proposal. Current to 22 October 2026.*

## What this adds

The main paper already covers Australia's early evidence, the French court ruling in outline, UK age-check numbers and the Texas app-store case. This annex goes country by country. For each one it asks four questions. What does the law require? Does it give parents a plan they can set? Is there evidence it works? What can Handrail learn? It also adds primary sources the main paper does not use. These include France's June 2026 regulator report on the parental controls that must come with every device, the full reasoning of the French Constitutional Council, the UK's July 2026 government response, and the parental control functions the Irish regulator's code now requires. Where I could only find a news report, or could not open a source, I say so.

A note on terms. A **parental control** is a setting that lets a parent limit what a child can do on a device or service. A **default** is how a setting starts before anyone changes it. **Age assurance** means any method of checking or estimating age.

## France: parental controls on every device (law 2022-300, the "loi Studer")

**What it requires.** Since 13 July 2024, makers of connected devices placed on the French market must include a parental control that is free and easy to reach. They must also offer to switch it on when the device is first set up. The state radio and spectrum agency, ANFR, checks compliance. Its test protocol checks five things: the offer at first set-up, the ability to block app downloads and apps that are banned for minors, that the control is free, that only the minimum data about the child is collected, and that the device comes with paperwork, including information on the health risks of screens ([ANFR, June 2026](https://www.anfr.fr/fileadmin/Controle_parental/Rapport_ANFR_Controle_Parental_vDEF.pdf)).

**How it has worked.** ANFR's first progress report, published in June 2026, is the best evidence. ANFR put 19 devices through its tests in 2024 and 2025 and had closed 11 cases by the end of 2025. Seven had no faults. Four had faults, and all four were fixed. ANFR says 73 per cent had no faults, which does not match its own count of 7 out of 11; the report does not explain the gap. Two cases led to formal notices and fines. The iPhone 16 did not always offer parental controls at set-up, in some cases involving minors over 15. Apple fixed this in iOS 26, released on 15 September 2025 ([ANFR, 25 November 2025](https://www.anfr.fr/en/anfr/news/all-news/detail-of-the-news/update-of-apple-iphone-16-following-a-non-compliance-related-to-parental-controls)). A Google Pixel 9 also needed an update ([ANFR, June 2026](https://www.anfr.fr/fileadmin/Controle_parental/Rapport_ANFR_Controle_Parental_vDEF.pdf)).

ANFR's own judgement is more useful than the pass rate. The report says:

- The law covers only content in app stores. The EU Digital Markets Act (DMA, the EU law on the largest tech firms) now allows other app stores and direct downloads, which in some cases tends to make that approach "obsolete".
- Controls can be got round "fairly easily", because a child can often set up the device alone and choose no control.
- Age ratings rest largely on what app makers say about their own apps. The same app can be rated differently in different stores. A web browser was rated PEGI 3 (suitable for all ages) on Google Play and 16+ on Apple's store.
- The maximum fine is €7,500, which ANFR calls weak. Its only other tool, taking a product off the market or recalling it, is often out of proportion.

ANFR recommends adding **limits on time of use**, blocking "developer mode", allowing **several profiles** on a shared device, a shortcut to the control settings, higher fines, and age ratings harmonised at EU or wider level. As good practice, it also suggests **an adult takes part in set-up** and ready-made profiles by age (under 7, 7 to 12, 13 to 15, 16 to 18). Elsewhere in the report it says the offer of controls should stay open until 18 ([ANFR, June 2026](https://www.anfr.fr/fileadmin/Controle_parental/Rapport_ANFR_Controle_Parental_vDEF.pdf)).

**Do parents use it?** I found no recent figure. The latest I found is from 2021: 56 per cent of parents of 5 to 17 year olds said they used some parental control ([Arcep, 16 March 2022](https://www.arcep.fr/actualites/actualites-et-communiques/detail/n/usages-numeriques-en-france-160322.html)). That was before the law took effect.

**Lesson for Handrail.** France has already made "offer a parental control at first set-up" a legal duty, and it can be enforced. That moment is where a Handrail plan should start. ANFR's wish list (time limits, an adult at set-up, profiles by age, ratings that match across stores) describes most of what a shared plan does. ANFR's finding that each device's own control leaks once apps come from elsewhere is the case for a plan that every service follows, not just the device.

## France: the 2023 "digital majority" law and the 2026 ruling

**The 2023 law.** Law 2023-566 of 7 July 2023 said that a child under 15 needed a parent's consent to join a social network. Parents could also ask for a child's existing account to be suspended. It never came into force. In a letter dated 14 August 2023, the European Commission said France had not notified it in time. It also said the law clashed with the Digital Services Act (DSA, the EU law that sets platform duties across the EU) and with the "country of origin" rule in the EU e-Commerce Directive (a platform answers to the country where it is based). A parliamentary review in February 2026 confirmed that no implementing decree was ever adopted ([Assemblée nationale, 3 February 2026](https://www.assemblee-nationale.fr/dyn/contenu/visualisation/1101356/file/Communication%20CACE%20application%20des%20lois%20f%C3%A9vrier%202026.pdf)).

**The 2026 law and ruling.** Parliament then passed an outright ban on social networks for under-15s. In decision 2026-911 DC, made public on 14 August 2026, the Constitutional Council struck out Article 1, the ban itself. The reasoning matters for Sweden. Paragraph 15 says that no provision lets parents, "duly informed of the potential risks and the safeguards" of a service, decide in the child's interest to **lift the ban, limit its reach, or allow access to certain services**. Paragraph 16 says the ban made no particular assessment of the risk to the child, taking account of age, maturity, family situation or the type of service. Paragraphs 20 and 21 add that the ban in effect forced everyone, adults included, to prove their age, and the law set no conditions or limits on how. I read the decision in a copy hosted by a Belgian legal website, not on the Council's own site ([Décision n° 2026-911 DC, 14 August 2026](https://www.droit-technologie.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/Decision-Conseil-constitutionnel.pdf)). The President asked the Prime Minister to work quickly on a "legally robust" version ([LCP, 14 August 2026](https://lcp.fr/actualites/reseaux-sociaux-pourquoi-le-conseil-constitutionnel-a-censure-l-interdiction-pour-les)). I found no new bill as of October 2026.

**Lesson for Handrail.** A constitutional court in an EU state has now said, in effect, that a blanket age ban needs a way for informed parents to adjust it. A certified plan is exactly that: the parent's informed, service-by-service decision, written down and followed. Sweden's legal tests are not France's, but Swedish drafters should read paragraphs 15 and 16.

## United Kingdom: children's codes and the new age-limit power

**Children's codes.** Under the Online Safety Act 2023, services had to follow Ofcom's Protection of Children Codes, or other effective measures, from 25 July 2025. The measures include strong age checks on services that allow harmful content, safer recommendation feeds and safe search ([techUK, 24 April 2025](https://www.techuk.org/insights/news/ofcom-publishes-protection-of-children-codes-of-practice-and-guidance)). Press coverage says there are more than 40 measures; this is unverified, as the page I read gives no count. That summary also mentions no duty on platforms to provide parental controls. I could not open Ofcom's own pages to confirm either point.

**The age-limit power.** The Children's Wellbeing and Schools Act 2026 inserted section 214A into the Online Safety Act. It lets the Secretary of State require services to block or restrict access for children up to a set age, including to **limit the time per day** they can use a service, to **limit the times of day**, and to restrict contact from strangers or location sharing ([legislation.gov.uk, section 70](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2026/21/section/70)).

**How it will be used.** The government response of July 2026 says it expects restrictions on social media services offering their services to under-16s to come into force in spring 2027, with the first regulations laid before Parliament by the end of 2026. For 16 and 17 year olds, platforms must switch on a **midnight to 6am curfew by default**, which the young person can switch off. The government chose a default rather than a forced rule partly because, in an Ofcom test on a mock platform, 95 per cent of participants kept default safety settings on. In pilots with about 300 families, teenagers and parents found an overnight curfew "a balanced and workable approach" among the three options tested; the pilots used 9pm to 7am. 84 per cent of full consultation responses agreed that parents should have control over their children's online experiences. On parental controls, though, the government's commitment is to "review the use of parental control tools" after the changes, alongside guidance and support for families. It says that, for under-16s, parents "will no longer need to rely on managing parental controls" on banned services. Wellcome will fund independent studies of the effect of the ban on young people's mental health ([DSIT, July 2026, CP 1643](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6a57d7ce31fb6daf31413797/NCP_Government_response_A.pdf)).

**Lesson for Handrail.** The UK has written time limits and curfews into law as things the state can require. Section 214A could carry a plan-shaped rule. The UK's own evidence that defaults hold supports Handrail's design, where the plan sets the defaults and the service follows them. The risk is the UK's assumption that a ban replaces parental controls. Children who get round the ban, which the main paper shows is common, then have neither.

## Australia: the under-16 law and its evaluation

**What it requires.** Since 10 December 2025, age-restricted platforms must take reasonable steps to stop under-16s holding accounts. There is no parental consent exception (see the main paper).

**Evidence.** eSafety's first evaluation report from its long-term study, published on 31 July 2026, found that under-16s holding accounts fell from 52.4 to 42.1 per cent, and any use fell from 85.9 to 81.5 per cent. It also found that **parents' awareness of their children's social media use had fallen** in some groups, especially girls and children aged 10 to 12. eSafety says it is "too early to meaningfully assess" whether the policy's aims have been met. The study follows over 4,000 children and families for two years ([eSafety media release via Medianet, 31 July 2026](https://newshub.medianet.com.au/2026/07/early-insights-from-esafetys-comprehensive-evaluation-project/164211/)). I could not open eSafety's own site, which timed out repeatedly. In September 2026 Parliament passed a bill widening eSafety's powers to demand information, including from age-check and app-store providers. MLex reports that the maximum fine doubles to A$109.2 million ([MLex, 14 September 2026](https://www.mlex.com/mlex/articles/2524610)). This differs from the A$99 million in the main paper, which was the figure announced in June. I could not check the final text.

**Lesson for Handrail.** The fall in parents' awareness is the cost the main paper predicts: a ban moves children out of their parents' sight. Any Swedish pilot should measure parents' awareness as a main outcome, as Australia now does.

## United States: app-store laws

**What they require.** Utah, Texas, Louisiana and Alabama have "app store accountability" laws. App stores must check the age of account holders, get a **parent's consent before a minor downloads or buys apps** or makes in-app purchases, and pass an age band and the consent to app makers through "age signals" ([Future of Privacy Forum, 3 June 2026](https://fpf.org/blog/comparing-enacted-app-store-accountability-acts/)).

**Court status, October 2026.**

- **Texas (SB 2420)** was blocked by a federal judge on 23 December 2025. The Fifth Circuit appeals court put that block on hold in late May and June 2026, so the law is in force. The Supreme Court declined to step in in July ([CCIA litigation page](https://ccianet.org/litigation/ccia-v-paxton/)). The appeal was argued on 4 August 2026 ([CCIA, 3 August 2026](https://ccianet.org/news/2026/08/ccia-presents-arguments-why-texas-app-store-law-likely-violates-first-amendment/)). I found no ruling yet.
- **Utah** amended its law in 2026 (HB 498). The main duties now start on 6 May 2027, and they can only be enforced by private lawsuits. The industry challenge was dropped on 21 April 2026 ([Loeb & Loeb, 1 May 2026](https://www.loeb.com/en/insights/passle/2026/05/update-on-utah-app-store-law--another-waiting-game)).
- **Louisiana** delayed its law to 1 July 2027 through HB 977, signed on 15 May 2026, which also added a "family account" exception ([Alston & Bird, 27 May 2026](https://www.alstonprivacy.com/?p=6021)).
- **California's Digital Age Assurance Act (AB 1043)**, signed on 13 October 2025, takes a different route from 1 January 2027. Operating systems must ask for the user's age at account set-up. A parent may enter it for a child, and **no verification is required**. Apps must treat the resulting age band (under 13, 13 to 15, 16 to 17, 18 and over) as the main signal of the user's age ([Kelley Drye, 21 October 2025](https://www.kelleydrye.com/viewpoints/blogs/ad-law-access/faq-digital-age-assurance-act-california-youth-safety-on-the-internet)).

**Evidence of effect.** None yet. Only Texas is live, and only since mid-2026.

**Lesson for Handrail.** These laws have already forced a channel from the parent, through the operating system or app store, to every app. Right now that channel carries only an age band and a yes or no. California shows it can work without ID checks. Handrail asks that the same channel carry the plan as well. The court fight in Texas is mainly about age verification and parental consent requirements, so a plan-based approach without ID avoids the issue the courts are most likely to strike down.

## China: one "minor mode" across devices and apps

**What it requires.** In November 2024 the Cyberspace Administration published a guide to building "minor mode". A user can switch minor mode on with **one action**, and all apps then switch with it. The device, the apps and app stores are linked ("three-party linkage"). The device, not each app, controls total daily time. The official Q&A says these limits are not "one size fits all" requirements or mandatory rules, and that the recommended times are defaults parents can adjust ([CAC, 15 November 2024](https://www.cac.gov.cn/2024-11/15/c_1733364304826443.htm)). As reported by Beijing News, the default is no service from 10pm to 6am, a break reminder after 30 minutes, and a recommended daily total of no more than one hour for under-16s and two hours for 16 and 17 year olds. Parents can exempt apps, and basic calls and messages, core system functions and registered education apps are exempt from the limits ([Beijing News, 15 November 2024](https://www.bjnews.com.cn/detail/1731661412129282.html)). The 2023 draft is said to have had a 40-minute default for under-8s; I did not check this against the draft text. The Beijing News report of the final guide gives only the two age tiers above, but I could not confirm from the full guide whether the under-8 tier was dropped.

**Evidence.** By September 2025, the National Computer Virus Emergency Response Center found that nearly 30,000 of 210,000 recently updated Android apps had a minor mode. A senior engineer there said breadth had begun to show but depth was lacking. There are no figures on how many families use it ([Xinhua, 23 September 2025](https://www.news.cn/politics/20250923/dfda17b3d5614070877750e1edd6ddd9/c.html)). On China's earlier gaming limits for minors, a study of over 7 billion hours of mobile game play found no fall in heavy gaming. The data did not include some major PC and console games (*Nature Human Behaviour*, 2023, reported by [SDU, 16 August 2023](https://www.sdu.dk/en/om-sdu/institutter-centre/mmmi_maersk_mckinney_moeller/news/studiesaartvivlomeffektenaflovgivningomskaermbrug)).

**Lesson for Handrail.** China is the only country that has built a single setting that the device, the app store and the apps all obey. That proves it can be done at national scale. But the state sets the content and the rules, so it is the opposite of Handrail's choice of controller. The weak uptake and the null result on gaming show that a cross-app mechanism alone does not change behaviour.

## South Korea: the repealed shutdown law

**What it required.** From November 2011, children under 16 could not play online games between midnight and 6am. In August 2021 the ministries for gender equality and for culture said the law was losing its effect, because many children had moved to mobile games it did not cover. They said they would rely on a system where **parents choose children's game time** ([Gigazine, 26 August 2021](https://gigazine.net/gsc_news/en/20210826-korea-abolition-game-curfew-law)). The National Assembly abolished the law in November 2021 ([Korea Herald, 16 November 2021](https://www.koreaherald.com/article/2725997)).

**Evidence.** A study by Choi and colleagues used national youth survey data from 2011 to 2015. It compared the change in groups the law covered with groups it did not (a "difference-in-differences" method). It found a fall in internet use in the first year that did not last, and no change in internet addiction or sleep ([Journal of Adolescent Health, 2018](https://www.jahonline.org/article/S1054-139X(17)30863-7/abstract)). I could not open the journal page and relied on the abstract as given in search results. In 2012, teenagers told a Korean newspaper that they could get round the law by using a parent's ID number or account ([JoongAng Daily, 27 January 2012](https://www.koreajoongangdaily.com/opinion/is-gaming-cinderella-policy-viable/11726615)).

**Lesson for Handrail.** One national curfew for everyone missed new devices, leaked through parents' accounts, and was replaced by parent-set time. Handrail starts where Korea ended up.

## Norway and Denmark: proposed age limits

**Norway.** The government consulted in 2025 on a social media age limit of 15 ([The Local, 10 June 2025](https://www.thelocal.no/20250610/norway-proposes-banning-social-media-for-under-15s)). After the consultation, access would begin on 1 January of the year a child turns 16, so that a whole school year starts together. The European Commission and the EFTA Surveillance Authority (which oversees EEA rules in Norway) responded positively in August 2026. A bill is due before the end of 2026, and the method for checking age is still open. This comes from a secondary source; I could not open the government's page ([Life in Norway, updated 15 August 2026](https://www.lifeinnorway.net/social-media-children-ban/)). I found no parental tool proposal.

**Denmark.** The November 2025 political agreement set a limit of 15, with some parents able to allow access from 13 "after a specific assessment" ([AP via ABC7, 7 November 2025](https://abc7news.com/post/denmarks-government-aims-ban-access-social-media-children-15/18127417/)). A draft bill went out for consultation from 7 September to 5 October 2026. It covers very large platforms that need a login, and is expected to take effect on 1 July 2027. It puts no sanctions on children or parents, and names the national ID wallet (AltID) as a possible age-check tool. The law-firm summary I read does not mention the route at 13 ([Bech-Bruun via Mondaq, 15 October 2026](https://www.mondaq.com/data-protection/1855422/new-age-limit-of-15-for-certain-social-media-platforms)). I could not see the bill text.

**Lesson for Handrail.** Denmark's route at 13 is the opening the French court asked for. But "a specific assessment" has no design yet. A certified plan could be the record of that assessment: what the parent allowed, on which service, and what the service did.

## Ireland: the Online Safety Code

**What it requires.** Section 14 of the code says a video-sharing platform that allows users under 16 must offer parental controls "under the control of the end-user". At a minimum, parents must be able to:

- stop a child seeing videos from users the child does not know;
- stop the child's own videos being seen by users the child does not know;
- filter content by words in its description or by data about the video;
- **set time limits on viewing**.

Platforms must explain the controls, draw users' attention to them, and **offer them to new users at sign-up** ([Coimisiún na Meán, Online Safety Code, October 2024](https://www.cnam.ie/app/uploads/2024/11/Coimisiun-na-Mean-Online-Safety-Code.pdf)).

**Evidence.** On 8 September 2026 the regulator opened its first formal investigation under the code, into X. It is examining whether X's parental controls are really in parents' hands, meet the minimum, are easy to find, and are offered at sign-up. Fines can reach €20 million or 10 per cent of turnover, whichever is greater ([Irish Times, 8 September 2026](https://irishtimes.com/technology/big-tech/2026/09/08/x-faces-irish-investigation-over-concerns-children-are-not-protected-from-pornography-and-violence); [Law Society Gazette, 9 September 2026](https://www.lawsociety.ie/gazette/top-stories/2026/september/cnams-first-probe-under-online-safety-code/)). There is no outcome yet.

**Lesson for Handrail.** Ireland is the closest precedent for what Handrail asks a regulator to do: write down the minimum parental control functions and check them. Many large platforms have their EU base in Ireland, so the Irish list is likely to reach many of them (my inference, not checked against the code's scope rules). Handrail's conformance levels could start from section 14.

## A note on Greece

ANFR's report points to Greece's Kids Wallet, a parental control app that needs an adult to sign in on a government website to switch it on ([ANFR, June 2026](https://www.anfr.fr/fileadmin/Controle_parental/Rapport_ANFR_Controle_Parental_vDEF.pdf)). It was built by the Ministry of Digital Governance ([Eurydice, 26 November 2025](https://eurydice.eacea.ec.europa.eu/news/greece-kids-wallet-parental-oversight-digital-age)). I found no uptake figures. It shows a government building the parent's tool itself rather than certifying others.

## Open questions

- Will France's redrafted under-15 law include the parental route that paragraph 15 of the Council's decision describes? Could a certified plan meet that test?
- Do EU rules leave room for national duties on devices, given ANFR's concern about other app stores and the Commission's 2023 objection to France's law?
- What did ANFR find on the devices still being processed (the report says nine), and does it now have current figures for how many parents use device controls?
- Will the UK regulations under section 214A, due by the end of 2026, include any duty to follow a parent's settings?
- Did China's final guide drop the 40-minute default for under-8s that the 2023 draft is said to have had?
- Will Denmark's final bill keep the parental route at 13, and how will the "specific assessment" work?
- When will the Fifth Circuit rule on the Texas law, and on what grounds?
- What is Australia's final maximum fine: A$99 million or A$109.2 million?
