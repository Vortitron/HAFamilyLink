# Annex: checking the Handrail paper's figures against primary sources

22 October 2026. A companion to *Handrail: a floor and a path*, Draft 0.5.

*Status: the corrections in Part 1 were applied to the paper and the deck on 22 October 2026. The smaller wording fixes in Part 2 have not all been applied yet.*

## What this adds

The backing paper says in its own text that several figures came from news reports and "must be re-read from source before publication". This annex does that. It lists the numbers and dated facts in the paper, finds the official or original document for each, and says whether the paper's wording holds. Where a news report was the only thing I could find, the table says so. Quotes are kept very short. Most rows paraphrase the source closely and give the page or section so the author can check. One finding changes the argument. The Australian eSafety figures on how children get round the ban describe only children who still held an account, not all children. The paper reads them as if they covered more children than they do. Four other rows contain factual errors. The rest need small wording changes or a better link.

How to read the status column:

- **Confirmed**: the primary source says what the paper says.
- **Differs**: the primary source says something different. A correction is given.
- **Only secondary found**: I could find only a news or law-firm report.
- **Not found**: I could not find support, or the page was blocked to me.
- **Unverified**: marked in the text where I could not open the source myself during this check.

Some official pages (eSafety, Ofcom, the Australian infrastructure department) blocked automated tools. I read them by opening them in a browser or by using official copies published on GOV.UK. Every link below is to a page or file I actually read on 22 October 2026, unless the row says otherwise.

## Part 1. The corrections that matter most

### 1. eSafety workaround figures describe account holders only

The paper says that "of children still on a restricted platform, about half were never asked their age, just over a third had an account that said they were over 16, about 11 per cent said a parent helped them, and 7.4 per cent used a VPN". The Summary repeats the parent figure as "about one in ten said a parent helped them get round it". A VPN (virtual private network) is an app that hides where a user is connecting from.

The primary report is eSafety's *Early days, early insights* three-month report, last updated 19 August 2026 ([eSafety report page](https://www.esafety.gov.au/research/social-media-age-restrictions-evaluation/early-days-early-insights-three-months-report); [PDF](https://www.esafety.gov.au/sites/default/files/2026-08/Early-days-Early-Insights-SMMA-Evaluation-Understanding-experiences-of-social-media-age-restrictions-August2026.pdf)). Its Table 4 (p. 16) covers only "children aged 10 to 15 who reported they held at least 1 age-restricted social media account" at three months (n = 357). The figures are 50.2 per cent said the platform had not yet asked them to verify their age, 37.1 per cent said the age on their account was 16 or over, 18.2 per cent said the platform's check got their age wrong, 12.0 per cent used another method such as make-up tricks or someone else's ID, 10.9 per cent said a parent helped, 8.5 per cent opened a new account after the platform shut the old one, and 7.4 per cent used a VPN. The shares add up to well over 100 per cent, so children could give more than one reason.

The difference matters. Using a platform (81.5 per cent) is not the same as holding an account (42.1 per cent). The report itself says several platforms can be used without logging in, and that its usage figures likely include use both with and without an account (p. 12). So "about one in ten" is one in ten of the account holders. As a rough share of all 10 to 15 year olds, that is nearer one in twenty. This is my arithmetic (10.9 per cent of 42.1 per cent), not eSafety's, and it assumes the subgroup matches the headline ownership figure.

**Corrected wording:** "Of children who still held an account three months in, about half said the platform had not yet asked their age, just over a third had an account listing them as 16 or over, about one in nine said a parent had helped them, and 7.4 per cent had used a VPN."

### 2. Messaging and gaming: frequency rose, not use

The paper says messaging-service use rose "from 40.5 to 52.3 per cent" and games "from 26.4 to 29 per cent". The report says 52.3 per cent used messaging platforms "daily or more frequently", against 40.5 per cent before the ban, and 29.0 per cent played video games online every day, against 26.4 per cent. It calls the rise in frequency of use of these other platforms statistically significant, meaning unlikely to be chance ([eSafety PDF, p. 19](https://www.esafety.gov.au/sites/default/files/2026-08/Early-days-Early-Insights-SMMA-Evaluation-Understanding-experiences-of-social-media-age-restrictions-August2026.pdf)). The same page says the frequency of social media use "remained unchanged". The paper's point survives, but the wording should say daily use.

### 3. Age assurance trial: the demographic finding is more mixed

Age assurance means any method of checking or estimating a user's age. The paper says face-scan estimates showed "worse accuracy for women, darker skin and Indigenous users". The trial's main report (Part A) says the systems "performed broadly consistently across demographic groups assessed". It says that despite an acknowledged gap in training data about Indigenous populations, it found no substantial difference in outcomes for First Nations and Torres Strait Islander Peoples. The age estimation volume (Part D, D.2.5) says some systems showed reduced accuracy for older adults, non-Caucasian users and female-presenting people near the age cut-off. For Indigenous users it records under-representation in the data used to train the systems, not a measured drop in accuracy ([Australian Government, trial page, published 31 August 2025](https://www.infrastructure.gov.au/department/media/publications/age-assurance-technology-trial-final-report); [Part A](https://www.infrastructure.gov.au/sites/default/files/documents/aatt_part_a_digital.pdf); [Part D](https://www.infrastructure.gov.au/sites/default/files/documents/aatt_part_d_digital.pdf)). The 8.5 per cent figure is right. Part D, D.10.9, gives false rejection rates at the 16+ gate of 8.5 per cent for 16 year olds and 2.6 per cent for 17 year olds. "Over-anticipating" is also right (Part A, finding on unnecessary data retention). The trial page also lists a 2026 corrigendum (a published list of corrections). I did not re-open it in this check.

**Corrected wording:** "...wrongly rejecting 8.5 per cent of 16 year olds at a check set at 16. Some systems were less accurate for non-Caucasian and female-presenting users near the cut-off, and Indigenous people were under-represented in the data used to train them."

### 4. Sweden's election result

The paper says the 13 September election "produced no majority". The Election Authority's final result of 19 September 2026 gives S 99, M 70, SD 62, V 30, C 25, KD 22, MP 22, L 19 seats ([Valmyndigheten, 19 September 2026](https://www.val.se/servicelankar/servicelankar/pressrum/nyheter--pressmeddelanden/pressmeddelande-nya/2026-09-19-valresultat-faststallt-i-2026-ars-riksdagsval)). S, V, C and MP together hold 176 of 349 seats, just over the 175 needed for a majority. The point the paper needs is that no government has been formed. On 15 October 2026 the Riksdag rejected Magdalena Andersson as Prime Minister by 248 votes to 99. The Riksdag notice says the Speaker may put forward up to four candidates, and that Ulf Kristersson remains Prime Minister of the caretaker government until a new one takes office ([Riksdagen, 15 October 2026](https://www.riksdagen.se/sv/aktuellt/aktuelltnotiser/2026/okt/15/nej-till-magdalena-andersson-s-som-statsminister_cmsd2ab1134-a03c-4be2-b345-af0aacff33d3sv/)). This may change in the coming days.

**Corrected wording:** "As of 22 October 2026 no new government has been formed. The Riksdag rejected the Speaker's first candidate for Prime Minister on 15 October, and the outgoing ministers remain in office as caretakers."

### 5. Sweden's DSA authority

The DSA is the EU Digital Services Act, the EU law on online platforms. The paper says Mediemyndigheten "is Sweden's DSA authority". The government named the Post and Telecom Authority (PTS) as Sweden's national coordinator under the DSA, the lead body. PTS is one of three competent authorities. Mediemyndigheten and Konsumentverket are the other two ([PTS, 9 February 2024](https://pts.se/nyheter-och-pressmeddelanden/pts-samordnare-och-behorig-myndighet-for-dsa-forordningen/)). This matters for the paper's second ask. PTS may be the body that has to recognise the mark.

**Corrected wording:** "Mediemyndigheten, under the culture ministry, is one of three Swedish authorities under the DSA. PTS is the national coordinator."

### 6. Two timings in the workaround section

- **Crikey.** The paper says parents were helping "from the first week". The Crikey article is dated 4 December 2025, six days *before* the law took effect on 10 December ([Crikey, 4 December 2025](https://www.crikey.com.au/?p=1230975)). Say "even before the law took effect".
- **Australian VPN chart.** The paper says Proton VPN rose from 174th to 19th "within days" of the March 2026 checks, and that "these figures come from Proton itself". The ranking comes from Sensor Tower, an app-market data firm, as reported by The Guardian. It is a ranking of free iPhone apps in Australia. The article does not give a start date for Proton's rise. It gives a ranking "as of Sunday", which was 8 March, the day *before* the adult-content checks began on Monday 9 March ([Guardian report republished by Daily Mirror, 9 March 2026](https://www.dailymirror.lk/international/Australians-turn-to-VPNs-after-adult-website-blocked-under-new-age-laws/107-334841)). The 2 March date in the same article refers to a different app. I could not open TechSpot (blocked). Only the UK 1,400 per cent figure comes from Proton itself ([Proton VPN on X, 25 July 2025](https://x.com/ProtonVPN/status/1948773319148245334)). **Unverified:** I saw this post only in a search listing and could not open it.

### 7. Budget figures need their real sources

- **eSafety.** The FOI (freedom of information) document confirms A$16.163 million for 2025/26 and A$3.810 million for 2024/25 ([eSafety FOI LOG 161](https://www.esafety.gov.au/sites/default/files/2026-01/LOG_161_Document_Set.pdf)). **Unverified in this check:** I did not re-open this file today. It does not contain the four-year A$45.7 million. That figure is in the 2024 to 25 Mid-Year Economic and Fiscal Outlook (MYEFO, the Australian Treasury's mid-year budget update). It funds eSafety's "regulatory oversight and enforcement functions" within A$76.1 million in total over four years for the social media minimum age ([MYEFO 2024 to 25, Appendix A, PDF page 291, printed page 283](https://archive.budget.gov.au/2024-25/myefo/download/myefo2024-25.pdf)).
- **Ofcom.** The £64.1 million for 2024/25 is confirmed but has no link in the paper. It is "total direct spend" on Online Safety Act duties in an Ofcom freedom of information reply of 9 January 2026. The same reply says Ofcom's Board agreed a budget of £72.6 million for the current year (2025/26) for costs directly attributed to online safety regulation ([Ofcom FOI, 9 January 2026](https://www.ofcom.org.uk/siteassets/resources/documents/about-ofcom/foi/2026/january/cost-vpn-personal-data-and-the-online-safety-act-age-verification.pdf)). Ofcom's audited accounts use a different basis. They show a £66.0 million online safety budget for 2024/25 (Table 4) and about £71.6 million cash operating expenditure for online safety in the costs-by-sector note ([Ofcom Annual Report 2024/25](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/686d22c410d550c668de3c61/Ofcom_Annual_Report_and_Accounts_2024_2025.pdf)). Use the FOI figure and name the basis.

### 8. Ofcom's view is a question, not a preference

The paper says "Ofcom's first age-assurance report already favours layered protection across devices, app stores and operating systems". The report says the protection of children "requires a system-wide effort". It says there are questions about the role that search engines, operating systems and app store providers can and should play. It promises a statutory app store report by January 2027 ([Ofcom, Report on the use of age assurance, 16 July 2026, Next steps](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6a56377e2f6185941a9a6493/Report_on_the_use_of_age_assurance.pdf)). Say "already asks what role operating systems and app stores should play".

The same Ofcom report also says "circumvention appears low", and notes that some activity may be missed because of reporting bias. The DSIT study found 39 per cent of 11 to 17 year olds had got round at least one check. The two reports measure different things, but a reader will notice the difference. The paper should say so before a critic does.

**Arithmetic note on the steward.** The steward is the body that would run Handrail. CSA lists certification at 2,000 USD, not 2,000 EUR as the paper says. Two members at the middle tier (2 × 21,500 USD) plus two certifications at 2,000 USD each comes to 47,000 USD, under 50,000 USD as the paper says. But covering 600,000 EUR from middle-tier dues alone needs about 30 members, not "roughly 25", at exchange rates near 1.1 USD to the euro. "Roughly 25" works only if some members pay the top tier. Say which mix is assumed.
