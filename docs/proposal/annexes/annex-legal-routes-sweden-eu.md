# Annex: the legal basis and legal routes for Handrail in Sweden and the EU

*Supplement to "Handrail: a floor and a path". Current to 22 October 2026. This is a map for officials, not legal advice. Points marked **[lawyer]** need a qualified EU or Swedish lawyer to confirm.*

## What this adds

The main paper makes two legal asks. In Sweden, one clause in the bill: a safe harbour (protection from penalties) for platforms that apply a certified plan. In Brussels, one ask on the KIDS Act. This annex checks both against the law. It has three findings.

First, EU law already asks for much of what Handrail does. The Commission's 2025 guidelines want tools for guardians that work on any device and fit "interoperable one-stop-shop tools". The KIDS Act proposal would make the largest platforms' tools for guardians work with tools from other companies.

Second, Sweden's own inquiry found that Sweden cannot put new duties, penalties or supervision on platforms covered by the Digital Services Act. So a Swedish law cannot protect platforms from penalties that the Commission or, for most large platforms, Irish authorities impose. The safe harbour belongs mainly at EU level.

Third, the right to move between controllers rests only partly on existing law. The standard has to promise the rest.

The annex also corrects one detail in the main paper. Mediemyndigheten supervises DSA Article 28(1) for platforms established in Sweden, but Post- och telestyrelsen (PTS) is Sweden's Digital Services Coordinator.

## 1. Digital Services Act, Article 28, and the 2025 guidelines

**The law.** The Digital Services Act (DSA, Regulation (EU) 2022/2065) applies directly in every member state. Article 28(1) requires online platforms "accessible to minors" to put in place "appropriate and proportionate measures" for a high level of privacy, safety and security. Article 28(2) bans adverts based on profiling when a platform is aware "with reasonable certainty" that the user is a minor. Article 28(3) says platforms do not have to process extra personal data to find out whether a user is a minor. Article 28(4) allows the Commission to issue guidelines ([DSA Article 28, unofficial copy of the final text](https://www.eu-digital-services-act.com/Digital_Services_Act_Article_28.html); official text on [EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2022/2065/oj/eng), which blocked automated reading).

Three more articles matter:

- **Article 35(1)(j)** names "parental control tools" as one way the very largest platforms can reduce systemic risks, meaning serious risks to society that their services create ([Article 35](https://www.eu-digital-services-act.com/Digital_Services_Act_Article_35.html)). These are the very large online platforms (VLOPs), with 45 million or more average monthly active users in the EU ([Article 33](https://www.eu-digital-services-act.com/Digital_Services_Act_Article_33.html)).
- **Article 44(1)(j)** says the Commission shall support voluntary standards for "targeted measures to protect minors online" ([Article 44](https://www.eu-digital-services-act.com/Digital_Services_Act_Article_44.html)).
- **Article 45** says the Commission and the European Board for Digital Services shall encourage voluntary EU codes of conduct ([Article 45](https://www.eu-digital-services-act.com/Digital_Services_Act_Article_45.html)).

**The guidelines.** The Commission published its guidelines on 14 July 2025 ([Commission, 14 July 2025](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/library/commission-publishes-guidelines-protection-minors)). The formal text is Communication C(2025) 6826 final of 7 October 2025 ([guidelines PDF](https://ec.europa.eu/newsroom/dae/redirection/document/118226)). The guidelines are not binding. But the Commission says it will use them as "a significant and meaningful benchmark" when it judges whether a platform meets Article 28(1) (paragraph 12). The part that matters most for Handrail is section 7.3, "Tools for guardians". In summary:

| Paragraph | What it says | What it means for Handrail |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 80 | Tools for guardians are an extra. A platform's compliance "must never rely exclusively" on them, partly because of split custody, foster care and absent parents | Handrail cannot replace a platform's own safety duties. The main paper already says the legal floor stays. |
| 81(a) | Tools should match the child's evolving capacities. They should be grounded in communication, learning and empowerment, not control | Matches the milestones and the parent view that narrows as the child grows |
| 81(c) | Tools should apply "regardless of the device or operating system" | This is Handrail's promise to work across vendors |
| 81(d) | The child gets a clear notice when tools are switched on, with safeguards against misuse by guardians | Matches the fixed notice shown to the child |
| 81(f) | Tools should be compatible with "interoperable one-stop-shop tools for guardians gathering all settings and tools" | The nearest thing in EU text to a Handrail controller |
| 82 | Tools may include default settings, screen time limits, seeing who the child talks to, and spending limits | Covers Handrail Levels 2 and 3 |

The guidelines must be reviewed "at the latest after a period of 12 months" (paragraph 92). I found no revised version published by 22 October 2026. The review is another way for Sweden to feed in. Paragraph 93 says the Commission will encourage Digital Services Coordinators, national authorities, researchers and civil society to contribute.

**Who supervises.** Under Article 56, the member state where a platform has its main establishment enforces the DSA. For the very large platforms, the Commission alone enforces the extra duties that apply only to them. It can also enforce their other duties, such as Article 28. The country of establishment can act on those only if the Commission has not opened proceedings on the same matter ([Article 56](https://www.eu-digital-services-act.com/Digital_Services_Act_Article_56.html)). Several of the largest platforms are established in Ireland. Google, Meta and TikTok, for example, brought the Google Ireland case (section 3) through their Irish companies ([judgment](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:62022CJ0376)). In Sweden, Mediemyndigheten supervises Article 28(1) for platforms established here. PTS is the Digital Services Coordinator, Sweden's national contact point for the DSA ([Förordning 2024:958, sections 4 and 6](https://data.riksdagen.se/dokument/sfs-2024-958.text); also [SOU 2026:35, p 180](https://www.regeringen.se/contentassets/d1b21d5210774bfba9797e08883c9633/sou-2026_35_pdf-a_webb.pdf)).

## 2. The KIDS Act proposal and how Sweden can feed in

**Status.** The Commission adopted the proposal on 17 September 2026. Its reference is COM(2026) 681, procedure 2026/0286(COD) ([proposal text, cover page](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:52026PC0681); [Commission library page, updated 13 October 2026](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/library/proposal-eu-kids-act-eu-keeping-internet-digital-spaces-accountable-and-trustworthy)). It is a regulation, a law that applies directly in every member state. Its legal basis is Article 114 of the Treaty on the Functioning of the EU, the internal market article ([proposal text, explanatory memorandum section 2](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:52026PC0681)). On 22 October 2026 the European Parliament's record showed the Internal Market committee (IMCO) in the lead and no rapporteur (the member who leads work on the file) listed yet. The stage was "preparatory phase in Parliament" ([Legislative Observatory, read 22 October 2026](https://oeil.europarl.europa.eu/oeil/en/procedure-file?reference=2026/0286(COD))). In the Council, the proposal is handled by the working party on audiovisual and media questions, where it was first presented on 2 October 2026 ([Government fact memorandum 2026/27:FPM10, 21 October 2026, sections 2.2 and 4.1](https://data.riksdagen.se/dokument/HE06FPM10)). The regulation would apply six months after it comes into force (Article 43). No date can be given yet.

**What the text says that matters for Handrail.**

- **Article 6(2).** Platforms "may allow" guardians to set up accounts with limited features for 13 and 14 year olds. Guardian tools are always on. The guardian can set a daily time limit of no more than one hour, and can approve new contacts in advance. "May" means platforms do not have to offer these accounts.
- **Article 20(1) to (4).** Social networks, video platforms, online games, AI companions and chatbots must offer tools for guardians. The tools must suit the child's age, be hard to get round and respect the child's agency and privacy. The child must be told when they are used.
- **Article 20(5).** VLOPs "shall ensure" their tools for guardians are "interoperable with tools for guardians provided by third parties", under the conditions in Article 6 of the Digital Markets Act, "where relevant". It is unclear what "where relevant" covers. **[lawyer]**
- **Article 20(7).** The Commission may set the technical and operational requirements for these tools by a delegated act. That is a binding rule the Commission adopts under powers the law gives it.
- **Article 23(3).** The Commission shall assess whether codes of conduct "are adequate to demonstrate compliance", and publish that assessment.
- **Article 26.** A platform can check that someone is the child's parent in three ways: signals from official state sources, signals the platform already holds, or, until a delegated act is adopted, the adult's own statement. The check must not be used to track or profile anyone. Under Article 31, each member state must give guardians at least one free, privacy-protecting electronic way to prove they are the parent.
- **Article 19(4).** Platforms must point minors to support and information, including resources and organisations at national and EU level. This supports Handrail's list of help services that can never be blocked, but does not require it.

Article 20(5) is Handrail's strongest footing in EU law. It turns tools for guardians that work across vendors into a duty for the largest platforms. It does not set a data format. An open, certified standard is one way to meet it.

**The Swedish government's first view.** The government's fact memorandum (faktapromemoria), its first written assessment of an EU proposal for the Riksdag, came from the Ministry of Culture (Kulturdepartementet) on 21 October 2026. It welcomes the proposal's aim and says it views positively that the Commission has proposed a common EU age limit. It judges the proposal compatible with subsidiarity. It says the Ministry plans to consult stakeholders in November 2026 ([2026/27:FPM10, sections 2.1, 2.4 and 3.2.1](https://data.riksdagen.se/dokument/HE06FPM10)). Note that the main paper places the age limit with the Ministry of Social Affairs. The EU file sits with the Ministry of Culture.

**How Sweden can feed in.**

1. **Public feedback** on the Commission's Have Your Say portal, open until 3 December 2026 ([Commission, KIDS Act FAQ, updated 8 October 2026](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/faqs/kids-act-explained); [feedback page](https://ec.europa.eu/info/law/better-regulation/have-your-say/initiatives/19418-Child-online-safety-keeping-digital-spaces-accountable-and-trustworthy-EU-Kids-Act-_en)).
2. **The Council**, where the Swedish government negotiates. Three amendments are worth proposing:
   - In Article 6(2), change "may allow" to "shall offer" for services in scope.
   - Extend Article 20(5) beyond VLOPs.
   - Say that a certified open standard is one way to meet Article 20(5) and the Article 20(7) delegated act.
3. **The Riksdag.** National parliaments have eight weeks to check a proposal against subsidiarity, the rule that the EU should act only where member states cannot do as well alone ([Justitieutskottet 2023/24:JuU1y](https://data.riksdagen.se/fil/D847A0F6-3ABD-479E-8660-BD512AD5881E)). The Riksdag sent COM(2026) 681 to the Committee on the Constitution (konstitutionsutskottet, KU) on 6 October 2026. Its record says the eight-week period for a reasoned opinion ends on 23 November 2026 ([Riksdag chamber record 2026/27:4, item 17](https://data.riksdagen.se/dokument/HE094)). KU began its check on 8 October and invited the Ministry of Culture to brief it on 22 October ([KU minutes 2026/27:2, items 14 and 15](https://data.riksdagen.se/dokument/HEA1KU2p)).
4. **The European Parliament**, through amendments in IMCO once a rapporteur is named.

## 3. A Swedish age law and the DSA

**What Sweden's inquiry concluded.** SOU 2026:35 concluded that a national age limit falls outside the area the DSA fully regulates. It also concluded that the limit is not a restriction under the e-Commerce Directive. But Sweden cannot write the law as a duty on platforms to check age. Nor can it set its own rules on how ages are checked, on penalties, or on supervision. The platforms' duty to respect the Swedish age comes from DSA Article 28 ([SOU 2026:35, section 10.3, pp 176 to 182](https://www.regeringen.se/contentassets/d1b21d5210774bfba9797e08883c9633/sou-2026_35_pdf-a_webb.pdf)).

The Commission's guidelines fit this approach. One of the cases where they say age verification is appropriate is a minimum age set by "Union or national law, in compliance with Union law", including for "specifically defined categories of online social media services" (paragraph 37(d)). Footnote 37 reminds member states of their duties under Directive 2015/1535. This is the TRIS procedure: a draft technical rule for online services goes to the Commission before it is adopted, and the country must then wait for a set period ([guidelines](https://ec.europa.eu/newsroom/dae/redirection/document/118226)).

**The country-of-origin rule.** Under this rule, an online service is regulated by the EU country where it is based. Article 3(2) of the e-Commerce Directive stops member states from restricting online services that come from another member state ([Directive 2000/31/EC](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32000L0031)). Article 3(4) allows a narrow exception for measures against one "given" service. In Google Ireland and others (C-376/22, 9 November 2023), the Court of Justice ruled that "general and abstract measures" aimed at a whole category of services do not fall within that exception ([judgment](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:62022CJ0376)). The inquiry shaped its proposal to avoid this problem.

**The Commission's view in practice.** On 6 July 2026 the Commission gave a detailed opinion on the French bill. Reports say it "did not expressly conclude" that the under-15 limit itself was incompatible with EU law. Its objection was to a separate national supervision system overlapping the DSA ([Tech Policy Press, 24 July 2026](https://www.techpolicy.press/what-frances-under15-social-media-ban-means-for-europe/)). The Commission extended France's waiting period to 10 August 2026 ([Reed Smith, 30 July 2026](https://www.reedsmith.com/articles/france-advances-legislation-prohibiting-social-media-access-for-under-15s/)). Both are secondary reports. I did not read the opinion itself.

**If the KIDS Act passes.** Recital 20, one of the explanatory paragraphs at the start of the proposal, says one EU minimum age of 15 is needed "to avoid fragmentation of the internal market by diverging national laws". The only exception it names is a higher national age for "specific categories of content such as pornographic or gambling content". I found no article that clearly allows or forbids a higher national age for social media. Sweden's rule, "the year the child turns 15", differs slightly from 15. A child born in December could join while still 14. **[lawyer: would the regulation replace the Swedish limit, and would the Swedish rule still apply in the gap?]**

**What this means for the Handrail clause.** A Swedish law cannot protect platforms from DSA penalties, because Sweden does not impose them on most of the platforms concerned. The main paper's clause needs rewording. What Sweden can decide is which accounts its own age rule covers. That is where a clause can sit (section 8).

## 4. GDPR Article 8 and Sweden's age of digital consent

GDPR Article 8 applies when an online service relies on a child's consent to use the child's personal data. The law calls such a service an "information society service": one usually paid for, provided at a distance, by electronic means, at the user's request. Below 16, a parent must give or approve the consent. Member states may lower the age, but not below 13. Services must make "reasonable efforts" to check that a parent consented, "taking into consideration available technology" ([GDPR Article 8](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32016R0679)). Sweden set the age at 13 in chapter 2, section 4 of the Data Protection Act (2018:218) ([Riksdagen, SFS 2018:218](https://www.riksdagen.se/sv/dokument-och-lagar/dokument/svensk-forfattningssamling/lag-2018218-med-kompletterande-bestammelser_sfs-2018-218/)).

Article 8 sets the legal ground for using data. It does not decide who may use a service, and many platforms rely on a contract instead of consent. For a child under 13, a plan on a parent's family account might count as evidence that a parent approved. Each platform has to judge that under GDPR. The standard cannot settle it. **[lawyer]** The KIDS Act says its age rules should apply "in parallel" with the age limits in GDPR Article 8, where a platform relies on consent (its Article 6(5)). So Sweden's age of 13 would still apply to consent for data, even if 15 became the account age.

## 5. Moving between controllers: what the law already gives

Four laws touch a family's move from one controller to another. None covers it fully.

| Law | What it gives | Gap for Handrail |
| --- | --- | --- |
| GDPR Article 20 | A right to get personal data "provided to a controller" in a "structured, commonly used and machine-readable format", and to have it sent directly "where technically feasible" ([GDPR](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32016R0679)) | Applies only to data used on the basis of consent or a contract, and only to data the person provided. That may leave out the history built up from it. The right belongs to the child; a parent would normally use it on the child's behalf. No common format is set. |
| Digital Markets Act (DMA) Article 6(9) | Gatekeepers (the largest platform companies, as designated by the Commission) must let users, and others users authorise, take their data with them, free and with "continuous and real-time access" ([DMA](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32022R1925)) | Applies only to designated services. The designated operating systems are iOS, iPadOS, Android and Windows ([Commission gatekeeper list](https://digital-markets-act.ec.europa.eu/gatekeepers_en)). It is unclear whether Screen Time or Family Link data is covered. **[lawyer]** |
| DMA Article 6(7) | Other companies get free, effective access to the same operating system features the gatekeeper's own services use | The strongest lever for an independent controller to reach Screen Time or Family Link. On 19 March 2025 the Commission adopted two decisions setting out how Apple must meet its interoperability duties: one on features for connected devices, one on how Apple must handle requests for access ([Commission, 19 March 2025](https://digital-markets-act.ec.europa.eu/commission-provides-guidance-under-digital-markets-act-facilitate-development-innovative-products-2025-03-19_en)). KIDS Act Article 20(5) refers to DMA Article 6, which includes this paragraph. |
| Data Act (Regulation (EU) 2023/2854) | Users of connected devices can have the device's data shared with a third party they choose (Article 5), from 12 September 2025 ([Data Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32023R2854)) | Recital 16 leaves out data created when a user records, displays or plays content, which is probably most screen time. Gatekeepers cannot receive data under it (Article 5(3)), so a family cannot use it to move into Family Link or Screen Time. The rules in Chapter VI on switching between data processing (cloud) services might cover a controller run as an online service. **[lawyer]** |

**Conclusion.** The standard can promise the right to move through certification, as the main paper does. But EU law does not yet give every family this right in full. An EU or Swedish law could close the gap by requiring tools for guardians to export and import data in a common format.

## 6. The UN Convention on the Rights of the Child and General Comment 25

Articles 1 to 42 of the Convention have been Swedish law since 1 January 2020, under Act 2018:1197 ([Riksdagen, SFS 2018:1197](https://www.riksdagen.se/sv/dokument-och-lagar/dokument/svensk-forfattningssamling/lag-20181197-om-forenta-nationernas-konvention_sfs-2018-1197/); the start date is from the unofficial collection at [lagen.nu](https://lagen.nu/2018:1197)). General Comment 25 (2021) is the UN Committee's guidance on how the Convention applies online. It is not part of the text made Swedish law. The main paper already cites some passages. Four more paragraphs bear on Handrail's design ([General Comment 25](https://www.ohchr.org/en/documents/general-comments-and-recommendations/general-comment-no-25-2021-childrens-rights-relation); the OHCHR page blocked automated reading, so the wording was checked against a copy of the official document, CRC/C/GC/25):

- **Paragraph 21.** States should make parents and carers aware of the need to respect children's "evolving autonomy, capacities and privacy".
- **Paragraph 39.** States should require businesses to meet "the highest standards of ethics, privacy and safety" in the design and running of their products.
- **Paragraph 59.** Restrictions such as filters must be "lawful, necessary and proportionate", with the reason explained to children.
- **Paragraph 76.** Monitoring tools, if not used carefully, "may prevent a child from accessing a helpline". This is the clearest basis for the list of help services that can never be blocked. It also says parents' monitoring "should be proportionate".

A Swedish bill must weigh the child's best interests (Article 3). Both the inquiry's choice to give parents no say and Handrail's approach can be tested against these paragraphs.

## 7. The EU AI Act and the optional AI guide

The AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689) was amended by the Digital Omnibus on AI (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744), published in the Official Journal on 24 July 2026 ([EUR-Lex, Regulation 2026/1744](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32026R1744)). This is how it applies to the guide:

- **Banned practices.** Article 5(1)(b) bans AI that exploits people's vulnerabilities "due to their age" to change their behaviour in a way likely to cause them "significant harm" ([AI Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32024R1689)). A guide that only suggests, inside limits a parent sets, is far from this. Even so, certification should test that a guide does not nudge the child to use screens more.
- **High-risk.** The Act lists high-risk uses in Annex III, which carry extra duties. Its education list covers admissions, grading, deciding a pupil's level, and watching for cheating in tests "in educational and vocational training institutions". A guide used at home falls outside this list, unless a school uses it to grade pupils. Since the Omnibus, the high-risk rules for Annex III apply from 2 December 2027 (amended Article 113).
- **Transparency.** Article 50(1) requires that people be told when they are dealing with an AI system, unless that is obvious. It applies from the AI Act's general date, 2 August 2026. The Omnibus did not move this duty; it only gives older systems until 2 December 2026 to meet a different duty, on marking AI-generated content (Article 50(2)). Handrail's rule that the child is told in plain words that a guide is on meets Article 50(1).
- **Parents as users.** Under Article 2(10), the Act's duties on people who use an AI system do not apply to individuals using it "in the course of a purely personal non-professional activity". The duties fall mainly on the company that supplies the guide, not on the family.
- **KIDS Act.** The proposal's chatbot rules cover "AI companions" and "general conversational chatbots". The definition of a general chatbot leaves out systems limited to "a specialised service, task or pre-defined set of functions". A guide that deals only with the plan is probably outside it, unless it acts out a relationship with the child, which would make it an AI companion. **[lawyer]**

## 8. Drafting the safe harbour: where it can sit

Here a safe harbour means that a platform meeting a set standard is treated as meeting its legal duty, or presumed to. The options are listed with the strongest first.

| Where it sits | Can it protect from penalties? | Comment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| KIDS Act delegated act, Article 20(7) | Nearly, if it adopts the standard's requirements | Binding across the EU. Needs a mature standard. |
| KIDS Act code of conduct, Article 23 | Partly. The Commission assesses whether a code is "adequate to demonstrate compliance". But recital 39 says taking part in a code does not "in itself presume compliance" | Best route in the near term. Handrail certification could be a commitment in the code. |
| DSA Article 45 code, or Article 44(1)(j) standard | No formal presumption, but it carries weight in practice **[lawyer]** | Available now |
| Review of the Article 28 guidelines | No. Following them does not guarantee compliance, but they are the yardstick | Paragraph 81(f) already points this way |
| Swedish law (the age-limit act) | Not from DSA penalties. It can decide which accounts Sweden's own rule covers | Draft wording below. Must go through TRIS. |
| Agency regulations (föreskrifter, binding rules issued by an agency) | Only within powers a law or ordinance gives it | An agency may issue rules only if the Riksdag or the government gives it the power (Instrument of Government, chapter 8, sections 1, 10 and 11, [Regeringsformen](https://data.riksdagen.se/dokument/sfs-1974-152.text)). It could publish the default plan and name recognised certifications. It could not add duties for platforms. |

**A possible Swedish clause** for the age-limit act. This is example wording only and has not been tested by a legislative drafter.

> *"An account held under a household plan that a guardian has applied through a certified controller, and that the provider applies in accordance with the certification, shall be regarded as an account set up by the guardian for the purposes of [the section that defines the accounts covered]. The Government, or the authority the Government appoints, may issue regulations on which certifications are recognised."*

This only defines which accounts Sweden's own rule covers. It creates no duty or penalty for platforms. It does reopen the inquiry's choice to give parents no say. A narrower version could recognise plans only for rules on content and time, not for letting a child in below the age limit. **[lawyer: whether a rule on scope like this counts as a requirement on services, under the inquiry's own test and under C-376/22.]**

**At EU level**, the ask before 3 December 2026 could be to add a sentence to Article 20. It could say that tools for guardians "shall be presumed to comply with paragraphs 1 to 5 where they conform to a standard or specification recognised under paragraph 7 or under a code of conduct assessed under Article 23(3)". This is proposed wording, not text from the proposal.

## Open questions

1. If the KIDS Act is adopted, would it replace Sweden's "year the child turns 15" rule? What happens to children who are 14 in that calendar year?
2. Can a Swedish clause that recognises certified plans pass the inquiry's own EU-law test and the Google Ireland judgment?
3. Does DMA Article 6(7) or 6(9) cover Screen Time and Family Link data? If so, an independent controller could demand access now.
4. Does any part of the Data Act, including Chapter VI on switching, cover a controller run as an online service?
5. Can a Handrail plan on a family account count as a parent's approval under GDPR Article 8 for children under 13?
6. Will the Committee on the Constitution agree with the government that the proposal respects subsidiarity before the 23 November 2026 deadline? Will the Ministry of Culture's November consultation be a route for the Handrail asks?
7. Has the Commission started the 12-month review of the Article 28 guidelines, and will there be a consultation?
